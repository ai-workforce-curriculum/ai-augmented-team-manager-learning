# Exercise 04 — Deferral Rubric Across Sibling Tracks

**Estimated effort:** 2 hours.

## Objective

Operationalise the three-question routing rubric from Chapter 4 into a **one-page team-specific deferral rubric**, then stress-test it against ten real asks pulled from your inbox, chat threads, or calendar from the past two weeks. The objective is to cut the time you spend deciding "is this mine?" from thirty seconds to five, and to catch the ambiguous 10% before you drift into the wrong neighbour's craft.

## Prerequisites

- Chapter 4 of this module.
- Exercise 01 complete (so you have real names for the neighbour boxes).
- Ten real asks from the past two weeks — Slack threads, email, 1:1 notes, a request from another team, a question from leadership. Include at least two you are genuinely unsure about.

## Steps

1. **Encode the three questions.** Write Chapter 4's three routing questions in your own words, specific to the names on your ladder (from Exercise 01). *(Is this at my team scope or beyond? Is it operational legwork for Alice (L15)? Is it peer craft for Bob (risk engineer, L25)?)* The point is that the names are concrete; abstract role names do not route a Monday-morning inbox fast.
2. **List the destinations.** Below the three questions, list five routing destinations with a one-line description each: *down · sideways · up · out · mine*. Each must have the real name(s) on your ladder attached.
3. **List the always-out categories.** Three bullets: *legal counsel · HR / employee relations · SOC / DFIR*. State what the signal usually looks like for each so you recognise it in-flight.
4. **Format as a one-pager.** Readable, printable, pinnable. If you keep a personal operating doc or a 1:1 template, add it there as a section.
5. **Run ten asks through the rubric.** For each of the ten:
   - A one-sentence summary of the ask.
   - The routing decision: *down to X · sideways to Y · up to Z · out · mine*.
   - The time it took to decide (self-reported; be honest).
   - A sentence on why — which of the three questions resolved it, or why it was ambiguous.
6. **Mark the ambiguous ones.** For any ask that took longer than 30 seconds or that you are still unsure about, write a second paragraph on the specific boundary you hit. Carry those into your next 1:1 with your manager or a peer manager to confirm.

## Starter guidance

- Use asks from the past two weeks, not hypothetical ones. The rubric only works if it survives real inputs.
- For asks that split (part yours, part a neighbour's), route the parts separately rather than claiming the whole.
- "Mine" is a valid routing decision — most asks that arrive to you *are* yours. The point of the rubric is not to maximise deferrals; it is to catch the ones you would otherwise take on wrongly.
- If you find yourself reaching for "mine" on every ask, pressure-test by asking: if I took three weeks of PTO, who would the ask fall to naturally? If the answer is a specific neighbour, it probably belongs to them.
- If three of your ten asks route to the same neighbour, that is a real signal: book a working session with them to clarify the standing ownership for that class.

## Acceptance criteria

The rubric is complete when:

- The three questions are stated in your own words, with real neighbour names from Exercise 01.
- The five destinations (down / sideways / up / out / mine) are listed with real names.
- The three always-out categories are called out with signal descriptions.
- The one-pager is saved and findable on your operating doc, wiki, or 1:1 template.
- Ten real asks have been routed through the rubric with summary, destination, time-to-decide, and reason.
- The ambiguous asks are flagged with a specific boundary note and a plan to confirm.

## Stretch goals

- Repeat the ten-ask drill a week later with a fresh set. Compare the average time-to-decide. Aim for under ten seconds per ask within a month.
- For each neighbour that showed up in the ten asks, draft a one-line "when to route to you" note — share with that neighbour and invite them to edit it. You have just co-authored a standing ownership statement.
- Pair with another L40 team manager at your company and swap rubrics. Capture one divergence in how each of you routes a specific class of ask; decide whether both are correct (different team contexts) or one of you is drifting.
