# Chapter 6 — Research Anchors: Josh Bersin AI-in-L&D and Microsoft Work Trend Index

> *When a VP asks "why is this programme shaped like this and not like the vendor's off-the-shelf curriculum," the manager who can cite one Bersin frame and one Work Trend Index finding wins the room. The manager who cites "best practices" loses it.*

## Motivation

R5 specifies the research grounding directly: *"grounded in Josh Bersin AI-in-L&D research and Microsoft Work Trend Index data."* These are the two public, citable research streams the posting language most often invokes when it names the shape a team-scope programme should take. Neither is peer-reviewed in the academic sense — Bersin is industry analyst research; MSFT Work Trend Index is a self-report-heavy annual survey — but both are *widely cited*, *public*, and *updated annually*, which is exactly what you need for the "defend the programme to leadership" leg of the role.

This chapter's job is to equip you to cite both correctly. mod-104 Chapter 5 teaches the same move for the academic empirical literature on AI-coding productivity; this chapter teaches it for the L&D / workforce literature that underwrites mod-105's artifact choices.

Two orientations before the detail:

- **Cite responsibly.** The failure mode is "Bersin says engineers are 40% more productive with AI." Bersin does not say that; what Bersin's work *does* say is more useful but less soundbite-friendly. Chapter 2 of mod-104 named the same failure for Peng et al.; the same discipline applies here.
- **Cite minimally.** You do not need to be an expert on Bersin's or Microsoft's full body of work. You need to know *what each source says* at the shape-level, *what it does not say*, and *where the primary source lives* so that an engineer or VP who challenges the cite can trace it.

## Core concepts

### Josh Bersin: AI-in-L&D and the capability-academy framing

**Who.** Josh Bersin is a widely-cited industry analyst on HR, learning, and talent strategy — formerly Bersin & Associates (acquired by Deloitte), now The Josh Bersin Company and the Josh Bersin Academy. His research team publishes regularly on how enterprise organisations are building AI capability in their workforces. The primary venues are the [Josh Bersin Company research site](https://joshbersin.com/) and the Josh Bersin Academy.

**What's cited in R5.** The posting language in the sample points at two operational concepts from Bersin's AI-in-L&D work:

- **The "capability academy" model.** Bersin's framing of a capability academy is that traditional course-based training does not produce capability on new fast-moving technologies; capability lands through a combination of *cohorted learning*, *applied artifacts in real work*, *community of practice*, and *an articulated map of what the learner now can do*. For details, the primary reference is the Josh Bersin Company capability-academy research and the Josh Bersin Academy's own programme design. mod-105's Chapter 2 cohort structure, Chapter 3 CoP, Chapter 5 artifact-based measurement, and Chapter 4 learning-stage pin are the capability-academy pattern applied to AI-tooling fluency specifically.
- **The AI-in-L&D posture.** Bersin's work on AI *inside* learning functions (how L&D leaders are using AI in their own practice, what that implies for capability building across the enterprise) argues that AI *shifts* capability-building from course-centric to practice-centric. This is the research-side underpinning of Chapter 5's "artifacts, not scores" position.

**What Bersin does *not* claim.** Bersin's research does not provide a quantitative "programmes shaped like X produce Y% more capability than programmes shaped like Z." The research is directional and framework-shaped, not experimental. A manager who wields a Bersin cite as if it were a controlled trial is over-claiming. The right form of citation is *"the capability-academy framing — cohort-based, artifact-producing, community-sustained — is Bersin's shape, and this programme implements that shape for AI-tooling fluency."*

**How to find and use the primary source.** The Bersin research site is the authoritative home. Many of the specific reports (capability academy research, AI-in-HR / AI-in-L&D annual reports) are member-gated; the Josh Bersin Academy and The Josh Bersin Company's public blog carry summaries of the frameworks that are sufficient for the shape-level citation this module needs. If you need to put the framework reference on a slide, cite the Josh Bersin Company research site and — if available — the specific report title and year.

### Microsoft / LinkedIn: the Work Trend Index

**Who.** Microsoft publishes the *Work Trend Index* annually — a mixed-methods report combining large-sample self-report survey data from global knowledge workers, product telemetry from Microsoft 365 and Copilot usage, and labour-market signal from LinkedIn. The 2023 edition was the first to make generative-AI usage in knowledge work a central theme; the 2024 and 2025 editions deepened the coverage. The authoritative home is [microsoft.com/worklab](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index).

**What's cited in R5.** The posting language points at Work Trend Index findings in two directions:

- **Where AI capability actually grows.** Work Trend Index data consistently reports that most self-reported productivity gain comes from users who have learned the tool *in the context of their actual work* rather than through generic AI-literacy training. This is the empirical shape that underwrites mod-105 Chapter 2's "real repo, real task" insistence and Chapter 5's artifact definitions.
- **The "AI power users" vs. "AI novices" distribution.** Work Trend Index editions have characterised an "AI power user" population that is small, cross-functional, and disproportionately generative of the productivity story. This is the data-side counterpart to Chapter 4's stage-4 engineers — a small cohort with disproportionate leverage on the team's pattern library.

**What Work Trend Index does *not* claim.** Three limits worth naming when citing:

- *It is self-report-heavy.* The productivity numbers are what users *say* they get. Microsoft also publishes some product-telemetry findings separately (e.g. research on Copilot acceptance behaviours) but the headline Work Trend Index productivity numbers are self-reported. Chapter 1 of mod-104 named the general problem of uncorrected self-report; the same caveat applies here.
- *It is a vendor-authored source.* Microsoft has a direct commercial interest in Copilot adoption. That does not make the data invalid, but it does make it a weaker evidentiary base than, say, a peer-reviewed academic paper. Cite with the vendor-authored caveat visible.
- *It is a cross-sectional survey, not a longitudinal study.* It describes the state of the market at the time of the survey. It is not a before-after causal measurement. An engineer who challenges "Work Trend Index says X" with "that's a snapshot, not a trend" is making a fair methodological point.

**How to find and use the primary source.** Work Trend Index editions are published on microsoft.com/worklab, free, downloadable, and dated. Cite the specific edition (year) when referencing a specific finding. The report typically includes an executive summary, several topic deep-dives, and methodology notes — the methodology notes are where you confirm sample size, countries covered, and self-report vs. telemetry sourcing for a given number.

### Why these two and not others

Three other sources that often get mentioned in the same conversation, and why mod-105 keeps the Bersin + Work Trend Index pair primary:

- **McKinsey State of AI / Deloitte AI survey.** Useful, cite-able, and often cited in exec contexts. Shaped more around *enterprise AI adoption* (what% of orgs are deploying, in which functions) than around *how people are learning the tools*. Secondary rather than primary for this module; see `resources.md`.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review AI research.** Peer-reviewed-adjacent, broader scope (strategy, governance, deployment). Useful for mod-110 and mod-112; secondary for mod-105.
- **Academic L&D literature (Kolb, Bloom, Wenger-Trayner).** Wenger-Trayner is directly cited in mod-105 Chapter 3 for the community-of-practice theoretical anchor. Kolb (experiential learning) and Bloom (taxonomy) inform the artifact-centric framing in Chapter 5 at the background level. They are not the R5-named anchors; keep them available as supporting references in [`resources.md`](resources.md).

The R5 posting language names Bersin and Work Trend Index specifically because these are what the market currently expects a team-manager to cite. That is the market-signal; follow it.

### How to put a cite on a one-pager without over-claiming

A worked example — the same claim written three ways, only the third of which is defensible.

> **Over-claim:** *"Bersin research shows this programme design produces 30% more AI capability than vendor-run training."*

Bersin does not publish that specific comparative number. Do not use.

> **Under-claim:** *"This programme is based on best practices."*

Uncitable. Treat "best practices" as a phrase to remove from your writing on this topic.

> **Right shape:** *"The programme design follows the capability-academy pattern from Josh Bersin Company research — cohort-based, artifact-producing, community-sustained — applied specifically to AI-tooling fluency. Microsoft Work Trend Index data (2024 edition) corroborates the central move: self-reported productivity gain is concentrated in users who have learned the tool in the context of their real work, not in users who have completed generic AI-literacy training."*

The third version names the source, the shape of the claim, the year of the specific edition, and the *scope* of the corroboration. It survives an engineer, VP, or governance partner asking "where did that come from."

### Cross-citing the academic literature from mod-104

mod-104 Chapter 5 teaches the citation practice for the academic empirical studies on AI-coding productivity (Peng et al., Ziegler et al., Noy & Zhang, Brynjolfsson-Li-Raymond, Dell'Acqua et al.). Those studies ground specific *artifact choices* in mod-105 Chapter 5 — each of the three artifacts (first-successful-PR, pair-with-agent, review-of-your-review) has a specific academic-literature anchor named in that chapter. In a one-pager that defends the mod-105 programme to leadership, the right practice is:

- **Bersin + Work Trend Index** for the *programme-level shape* (why it is a programme with cohorts, artifacts, CoP, not a workshop).
- **Peng, Ziegler, Noy-Zhang, Brynjolfsson, Dell'Acqua** for the *artifact-level choices* (why the three specific artifacts).

Two layers of citation, one coherent frame. A manager who conflates the two — citing Peng et al. for the CoP structure or Bersin for a 55% productivity number — loses the room.

### A reading plan for the research anchors (half-day budget)

A focused half-day gets you to the floor of citation fluency on these sources:

- **90 minutes — Josh Bersin Company site.** Read the summary pages on the capability academy framing and on AI-in-L&D. Note the frame vocabulary that recurs (academy, cohort, capability, journey, capability-architecture). You will reuse this vocabulary in the exec pre-read.
- **90 minutes — most recent Work Trend Index edition.** Read the executive summary and the knowledge-work / AI section in full. Read the methodology notes. Pull out *two specific findings* you would cite in a one-pager and note the page/section numbers.
- **60 minutes — previous Work Trend Index edition.** Compare the AI findings year-on-year. If a finding changed, note which direction and by how much. Year-on-year change is itself a cite-able signal.
- **60 minutes — write your own two-paragraph framing.** Produce the Bersin + WTI framing paragraph (the "right shape" paragraph above, in your own words, specific to your programme).

Three and a half hours. This is the reading baseline a mod-105 manager holds; refresh annually when the next Work Trend Index edition drops.

### What this chapter is *not*

- **Not an academic lit review.** It equips you to cite industry research responsibly; it does not pretend to be a peer-reviewed survey. The peer-reviewed literature on AI productivity in software engineering is covered in `mod-104` Chapter 5.
- **Not a Bersin endorsement.** The Josh Bersin Company produces useful shape-level frameworks; it does not produce causal evidence. The chapter's recommendation is to cite the frame, not to rely on the brand.
- **Not a Microsoft advocacy document.** The Work Trend Index is cite-able and useful; it is also vendor-authored. The chapter's recommendation is to carry the vendor caveat on the cite, not to avoid the source.

### Common mistakes when citing

- **Treating a shape-level frame as a quantitative claim.** Bersin's capability-academy framing is directional; citing it as if it is "proven to produce N% gain" misuses the source.
- **Citing the Work Trend Index headline number without the methodology caveat.** "Work Trend Index says 70% of knowledge workers use AI" without the sample, year, and self-report caveat is fragile and will be challenged.
- **Conflating industry research with academic research.** Bersin is industry analysis; Peng et al. is a peer-reviewed study. Treating them as the same category of evidence is a credibility hit.
- **Letting the citation list grow.** A one-pager with 12 cited sources is less persuasive than one with 3 well-chosen cites. Pick two framework-level cites (Bersin + Wenger-Trayner on CoP) and two data-level cites (Work Trend Index + one academic study per mod-104 Chapter 5) per one-pager.

## Example: a one-pager paragraph using both anchors

Treat as calibration, not a template.

> **Why this programme is shaped the way it is.** The programme design follows the capability-academy pattern from Josh Bersin Company research — cohort-based (3–6 engineers per cohort, mixed learning stage), artifact-producing (first-successful-PR-with-tool, pair-with-agent reflection, review-of-your-review), community-sustained (bi-weekly CoP show-and-tell, team pattern library). Microsoft's Work Trend Index (most recent edition) corroborates the central move: self-reported productivity gain is concentrated in users who have learned the tool in the context of their real work, not in users who have completed generic AI-literacy training. We therefore run bootcamps on the team's actual repository, measure fluency via artifacts produced on real work items, and resist the one-shot generic-training pattern. The academic grounding for the three measurement artifacts — Peng et al. for the first-successful-PR shape, Dell'Acqua et al. for the pair-with-agent framing, Brynjolfsson-Li-Raymond for the longitudinal self-review — is detailed in the mod-104 evidence pack and is cited at the artifact level in the measurement rubric.

One paragraph, two framework-level cites, named academic cites deferred to their specific artifact context, no over-claim.

## Summary

- Two research anchors R5 names explicitly: **Josh Bersin Company** AI-in-L&D / capability-academy framing, and **Microsoft Work Trend Index** (annual).
- Bersin: shape-level, directional, framework-shaped. Use to justify the programme *shape* (cohort, artifact, CoP, learning-stage).
- Work Trend Index: cross-sectional, self-report-heavy, vendor-authored. Use to corroborate the claim that capability lands in *context of real work*.
- Cite responsibly: name the source, name the year, name the caveat (self-report; vendor-authored; cross-sectional for WTI; directional for Bersin).
- Do **not** conflate industry framework-level sources with peer-reviewed academic studies. Use the mod-104 Chapter 5 studies for the artifact-level claims; use Bersin + WTI for the programme-level shape.
- Half-day reading plan: 90 min Bersin summary; 90 min most recent Work Trend Index; 60 min prior edition for year-on-year comparison; 60 min write your own framing paragraph.
- Keep citation count low: ~4 well-chosen cites per one-pager.
- Common mistakes: shape-as-quantitative; headline-number without caveat; industry-as-academic; citation bloat.
