# Chapter 8 — Ownership Boundary: Manager Framing, HR Formal Processes

> *The carve-outs, the ladder, the 1:1 prompts — all sit on the manager's side of the line. Performance improvement plans, terminations, and formal employment actions sit on HR's side of the line. This chapter names the line and the three handoffs across it so the manager does neither more nor less than their job.*

## Motivation

Every module in this track names the boundary against adjacent domains in a closing chapter, because the level-40 team-manager role lives at the intersection of several specialties and overreach is one of the common failure modes. For mod-107, the specific boundary to name is with HR / employee-relations partners.

The reason the boundary matters here is that the atrophy work has a near neighbour the manager must not mistake it for: formal performance management. An IC who shows a settled deference pattern despite two quarters of carve-outs, 1:1 interventions, and ladder re-contextualisation is in a territory where formal process might be appropriate. *Might be*. The decision to open a formal performance improvement plan is not the manager's to make alone. It is a joint decision with HR, and the mechanics — the documented plan, the review cadence, the review criteria, the possible terminal outcomes — are HR-owned.

This chapter names what the manager does, what HR does, and where the three handoffs across the boundary are.

## Core concepts

### What stays on the manager's side

- **Observing patterns in 1:1s and retros.** The Chapter 7 prompts are the manager's instrument. The observations are the manager's responsibility to keep.
- **Running the carve-outs.** Chapter 5's scheduled interventions are entirely manager-side.
- **The ladder and the IDP.** Chapter 6's ladder update and Exercise 05's IDP template are team-scope artifacts the manager owns.
- **Growth conversations in 1:1s.** The hard-case conversation in Chapter 7 (the settled deference pattern) is a growth conversation. Growth conversations are the manager's work.
- **Documenting observations privately.** Manager 1:1 notes, retro observations, and the manager's own calibration notes are personal-manager records. They are not HR records until a formal process begins.
- **Advocating in calibration meetings.** Comp calibration and promotion calibration conversations are manager-side; the ladder authored in Chapter 6 is the standard.
- **Celebrating judgement moves in retros.** The positive side of the Chapter 7 prompts — surfacing and naming the IC's successful judgement calls — is a manager move, not an HR one.

### What belongs to HR

- **Performance improvement plans (PIPs).** The documented plan with specific criteria, timeline, review cadence, and consequences is HR-owned. In most organisations the manager drafts with HR and HR approves and holds the process; the specific procedure is organisation-dependent.
- **Terminations.** Deciding, documenting, and executing a termination is HR-owned work. Even in organisations where the manager delivers the termination conversation, the decision-and-documentation sits in HR.
- **Compensation calibration at scale.** Individual calibration arguments are manager-side (above); the calibration process, the band structure, and the enforcement of equity across teams are HR-owned.
- **Formal performance-review process mechanics.** The review template, the required fields, the signature flows, the storage of the reviews — HR-owned.
- **Levelling and promotion process mechanics.** The promotion-committee structure, the quorum rules, the appeal process — HR-owned. Content of the promotion case is manager-authored; process is HR-owned.
- **Legal and employment-law exposure assessments.** Any question that begins *"could this expose us to a claim?"* belongs to HR and, through HR, to counsel. Not to the manager.
- **Formal mediation of interpersonal conflicts that cross the line into employment issues.** Team-dynamics conflicts the manager handles; employment-relations conflicts (harassment claims, protected-class considerations, retaliation concerns) belong to HR.

If an item on this list is on the manager's plate because the organisation has unusual boundaries, the right move is not to accept the mis-assignment; it is to clarify with HR what the organisation's intent is and get the ownership written down.

### The three handoffs across the boundary

The handoffs are where the atrophy work can mis-route if the boundary is not held.

**Handoff 1 — the "I've tried the Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 interventions and the pattern hasn't shifted" conversation.** This is the handoff from manager-side growth work to joint HR partnership. The manager brings:

- Two quarters of documented observations (specific 1:1 notes, retro content, outcomes of the Chapter 5 carve-outs for this IC).
- A specific description of what *has* been tried and what the signal has been.
- A specific question: *"is this a growth-conversation pattern I should continue to work on in 1:1s, or is this now a conversation we should structure differently?"*

The question is *open*; the manager does not pre-decide the answer. HR's role in the conversation is to assess, with the manager, whether a formal process is appropriate — and to say, as often as not, *"keep working on it in 1:1s for another quarter, and here's a way to structure the growth conversation more sharply."* Opening a formal process is one possible outcome; it is not the default.

**Handoff 2 — the incident-connected handoff.** When an incident post-mortem (mod-109) surfaces that an IC's AI-deference pattern was a contributing factor, two things happen in parallel: the mod-109 blameless post-mortem runs (and the IC participates in it like any other contributor), and the manager — separately, outside the post-mortem — decides whether the pattern this IC demonstrated is a repeated pattern worth bringing to HR under Handoff 1. The two tracks do not mix: the post-mortem is not evidence for a performance process, and the performance conversation is not a continuation of the post-mortem.

mod-109 is explicit on the blameless frame; mod-107's addition is that the manager's *separate* assessment of whether a pattern is forming is not a violation of blamelessness. Blamelessness governs the post-mortem; growth assessment is what the manager does with every IC on the team continuously.

**Handoff 3 — the formal-process-begins handoff.** When the Handoff-1 conversation results in a joint decision to open a formal process, the character of the manager's work changes. The 1:1 content that was personal-manager notes becomes material the manager coordinates with HR on. The growth conversation becomes a documented review conversation. The Chapter 7 prompts no longer fit the situation — a formal process is not a psychological-safety-building exercise.

Three things the manager must do once Handoff 3 happens:

- Stop documenting 1:1 content in the same personal-notes format; follow HR's documentation protocol instead.
- Communicate to the IC that the conversation character has changed. (HR will typically own the specific communication; the manager participates per the organisation's process.)
- Protect the rest of the team's psychological safety by continuing to run the Chapter 5 carve-outs, the Chapter 6 ladder conversations, and the Chapter 7 prompts *for everyone else* normally. A single IC on a formal process does not change the diagnostic work the team's other ICs need.

### What the manager must *not* do

- **Do not run an informal PIP.** Scheduling weekly check-ins with explicit improvement milestones, written warnings, or performance-contingent consequences is a PIP whether it is called one or not. Running one informally — without HR process — is both ineffective and exposes the organisation and the manager. If a formal process is warranted, open one through Handoff 1.
- **Do not threaten consequences.** *"If this pattern doesn't change by next quarter, we'll have to talk about your role"* is a threat and reads as one. The growth conversation names the pattern and the work; the consequence conversation is HR's, after Handoff 1.
- **Do not bypass HR on terminations.** Even in organisations where the manager has nominal authority to terminate, the right move is to coordinate with HR first. Termination without documentation is the single most common source of legal and cultural exposure in this territory.
- **Do not use the Chapter 7 prompts to build a case.** The prompts are diagnostic and growth-oriented; repurposing them as evidence for a performance process breaks psychological safety for the IC and, if they realise the shift, for the entire team.
- **Do not cite Dell'Acqua or Noy & Zhang as justification in an HR conversation about an individual IC.** The research is about populations; an individual's pattern is specific. The research is justification for *the carve-outs and the ladder*; it is not evidence that *this particular IC* is deferring.

### What to do when you're not sure

The "not sure" case is more common than either the clear growth-conversation case or the clear formal-process case. A few principles:

- **Default to another quarter of growth conversation.** A single-quarter signal is noisy; patterns are two-quarter signals. Opening a formal process on a one-quarter observation is almost always premature.
- **Consult HR on the pattern, not on the IC.** A conversation with HR that asks *"what does the process look like for a pattern of X; is this the kind of thing that warrants it?"* is fine and useful. A conversation that names the IC and asks for a recommendation is Handoff 1.
- **Check whether the Chapter 5 carve-outs actually ran.** If Carve-out 2 (AI-off artifact) was skipped three quarters in a row, the IC has not had the intervention. Running it is not optional before considering a formal process.
- **Check for mitigating factors.** A pattern in an IC who returned from leave, took on a new scope, is on a new team, or experienced a personal event is not the same as a steady-state pattern. Chapter 5's interventions assume baseline conditions.
- **Ask a senior manager or a peer in the organisation.** The second-pair-of-eyes check is specifically useful on boundary questions. If the organisation has an internal culture of manager peer-review (often around calibration meetings), the atrophy-pattern question fits that forum.

### How this boundary interacts with mod-108 and mod-109

- **mod-108 (hiring, performance, feedback).** mod-108 runs the ongoing performance-review mechanics. mod-107's ladder is the standard mod-108 reviews against. mod-108's performance-review template, review cadence, and feedback-cycle design are mod-108 content, not HR-owned (unless your organisation runs the review template centrally, which many do — compose accordingly).
- **mod-109 (accountability, incident response, blameless post-mortems).** mod-109 runs the post-mortem that catches the incident-level consequence of the deference pattern. mod-107's manager-side assessment of whether the pattern is forming is separate (Handoff 2 above).
- **mod-110 (governance and policy at team scope).** Policy at team scope is where documentation requirements live. If the organisation has a written AI-use policy that intersects with performance expectations (e.g., required disclosure of AI-assisted work), mod-110 is where that interaction is navigated; mod-107's role is only to keep the atrophy-diagnostic work consistent with the policy.

### The upward boundary: when mod-107 hands off to L45 / L50+

- **Org-wide skill-growth programming.** If the atrophy work needs to be done at scale across many teams (10+ teams in a division, a company-wide capability-academy for AI judgement), the scope exceeds a single team manager. [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) (level 45) is the owner; mod-107 feeds signal upward, does not run the org-wide programme.
- **Engineering-manager-of-managers scope.** When a manager of managers is designing growth standards *for other managers*, the scope is [`ai-engineering-manager`](https://github.com/ai-engineering-curriculum/ai-engineering-manager-learning) (level 50). mod-107 is single-team scope.
- **Policy authoring that goes beyond team scope.** AI-policy work that will govern how performance reviews, promotions, or formal processes incorporate AI usage across the organisation belongs to `head-of-ai-governance` (level 60) through HR-leadership partnership.

### Common failure modes at the boundary

- **The manager runs an informal PIP and calls it a growth plan.** The IC experiences the mismatch and loses trust; the organisation has no documentation when the pattern persists.
- **The manager escalates to HR too fast.** A pattern that Chapter 5's carve-outs would have resolved becomes a formal process; the signal is noise at that scale and the formal process produces a bad outcome.
- **The manager escalates too slowly.** A pattern that has persisted for four quarters of growth conversation is a pattern the growth conversation is not going to resolve; the manager owes the IC the clarity of a formal process at that point, and owes HR the signal.
- **The manager uses HR as an outlet, not a partner.** Conversations about team members with HR should be about process and framing, not venting. The partner relationship is the resource; the venting relationship corrupts it.
- **The manager hides behind HR to avoid a growth conversation.** The reverse failure — *"HR told me we can't do X"* as a way to avoid saying what the manager thinks. Own the growth conversation; escalate when the boundary is actually crossed.

## Example: a two-quarter growth conversation that stayed on the manager's side

Composite calibration example; not a case study.

- **Quarter 1.** The 1:1 prompts surface a consistent pattern: IC cannot name a rejection of an AI suggestion, deferred on the AI-off carve-out artifact (produced unusable output; did not seem alarmed), and showed difficulty on one outside-frontier task-type in paired review. Manager runs Chapter 5 Carve-out 2 and 3 for the IC specifically; has the growth conversation per Chapter 7 ("here's the pattern I see; here's the ladder axis we need to grow on; here's the next quarter's carve-out; let's revisit in 12 weeks").
- **Quarter 2.** The pattern has partly shifted. The IC named a rejection in a 1:1 (modest but real); the next AI-off artifact was closer to what the baseline level would require; paired review surfaced one clear judgement move. Manager continues Chapter 7 and Chapter 5 work, documents the progress in 1:1 notes, closes the quarter with explicit recognition of the direction of change.
- **Decision point:** growth conversation continues, not escalated. The pattern is shifting under the intervention. A formal process would have been a mistake here.

A different version of the example — the pattern does not shift at all over two quarters, the IC seems unconcerned, and the carve-outs have run twice with no signal of change — is the Handoff-1 conversation. The manager opens it with HR by naming the pattern, the interventions, and the question (*"is this the kind of pattern that warrants a different conversation?"*). HR decides with the manager; the ownership of the decision is shared.

## Summary

- The boundary with HR is **sharp and holds in both directions**: the carve-outs, ladder, and 1:1/retro diagnostic work are manager-side; formal employment actions (PIPs, terminations, compensation calibration at scale) are HR-side.
- **Three handoffs** across the boundary: (1) "two quarters of growth work hasn't shifted the pattern" — open the conversation with HR as a question, not a recommendation; (2) incident-connected handoff — mod-109 post-mortem runs blameless and separate; manager's growth assessment is a separate, parallel conversation; (3) formal-process-begins handoff — documentation protocol shifts, communication is HR-owned, carve-outs continue normally for everyone else.
- **Three things the manager must not do:** run an informal PIP, threaten consequences in growth conversations, bypass HR on terminations.
- **The "not sure" case is the common case.** Default to another quarter of growth work; consult HR on the process pattern, not on the individual; verify the carve-outs actually ran; check for mitigating factors; get a second pair of eyes.
- **Composes with** mod-108 (performance-review mechanics run against the mod-107 ladder), mod-109 (post-mortem stays blameless; pattern assessment is parallel), mod-110 (policy intersections for AI-use disclosure and the like).
- **Upward boundary:** org-wide skill-growth programming goes to [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) (level 45); manager-of-managers scope goes to [`ai-engineering-manager`](https://github.com/ai-engineering-curriculum/ai-engineering-manager-learning) (level 50); cross-org policy goes to `head-of-ai-governance` (level 60) through HR leadership.
- The sign that the boundary is holding is that your HR partner respects the growth work you do on your side, and you respect the formal process when it is theirs to own.
