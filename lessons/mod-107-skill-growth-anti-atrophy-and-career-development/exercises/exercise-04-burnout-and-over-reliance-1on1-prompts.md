# Exercise 04 — Burnout and Over-Reliance 1:1 Prompts

**Estimated effort:** 2 hours.

## Objective

Produce **the working set of 1:1 and retro prompts** your team's manager will use to surface deference, dependency, burnout, and team-level AI-wrong signal — grounded in [Amy Edmondson's psychological-safety construct](https://fearlessorganization.com/the-fearless-organization-book) and the [Westrum culture typology (BMJ 2004)](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/13/suppl_2/ii22). The artifact is a 2-page prompt bank organised by category, annotated with when to ask, what to listen for, and how to follow up — plus a rotation guide so no IC experiences the 1:1 as an atrophy audit.

This is the diagnostic instrument Chapter 7 names. It is specifically designed to *replace* the annual engagement survey's role in detecting the atrophy signal with something immediate, attributed, and actionable.

## Prerequisites

- [Chapter 7](../07-instrumenting-burnout-and-over-reliance.md) — the four categories of prompts, the Edmondson and Westrum groundings, and the common failure modes.
- The policy memo from [Exercise 01](exercise-01-empirical-research-into-team-policy.md) — the research citations the prompts' framing leans on if challenged.
- A functioning weekly 1:1 cadence with your direct reports. If 1:1s are not weekly or not functioning, the prompts cannot do their job — fix the 1:1 practice first (General management reading list in [`PREREQUISITES.md`](../../../PREREQUISITES.md)).
- A functioning sprint or fortnightly retro practice at team level. If retros are not happening regularly, Category 4 of the prompts has nowhere to land.
- Optional: a recent-reading of Edmondson *The Fearless Organization* (or the 1999 ASQ paper); the Westrum 2004 BMJ paper.

## Problem statement

The atrophy signal does not announce itself on the dashboards. It lives in the specifics of conversations: *"I accepted the AI's suggestion because I was running short on time"* / *"I couldn't name a case where the AI was wrong and I caught it"* / *"I feel like I'm doing more review of AI-assisted code than engineering, and it is tiring."* These sentences do not appear in an engagement survey, where they would be aggregated and anonymised. They appear in 1:1s and retros — but only if the manager asks the right question at the right cadence, and only if the team feels safe enough to answer honestly.

The prompt bank is the operational response. It gives the manager a working set of prompts organised by the signal they are meant to surface, calibrated to a rotation that does not fatigue the 1:1, and annotated so the manager knows what to listen for and how to follow through. It is also a defence against the common failure of *asking every 1:1 the same question until nobody is listening anymore*.

## Steps

1. **Draft the four categories of prompts.** Use Chapter 7's structure as the scaffold. For each category, write 4–6 specific prompt variants (so the manager has a rotation within the category):
   - **Category 1 — deference prompts** (addresses Dell'Acqua). Variants that probe for AI-rejection, AI-modification, and honest self-assessment on outside-frontier task-types.
   - **Category 2 — dependency prompts** (addresses Brynjolfsson). Variants that surface the IC's model of what they have delegated and whether they can still do it.
   - **Category 3 — burnout prompts** (addresses the attention-shift risk). Variants that surface the post-automation attention trade and the cognitive-cost-of-judgement risk.
   - **Category 4 — retro prompts** (team-level; added to the existing retro). Variants that surface team-level AI-wrong signal, celebrate judgement calls, and feed the next quarter's carve-out scope.
2. **Annotate each prompt with three fields.**
   - *When to ask.* Which IC seniority, which stage of the 1:1 (opening vs. deep), which rotation slot. Not every prompt fits every IC.
   - *What to listen for.* The specific signal — the content, the hesitation, the vocabulary. A one-line note per prompt on what a growth answer sounds like and what a deference answer sounds like.
   - *How to follow up.* The next 1:1's return question. *"Last week you mentioned X; what came of it?"* The follow-up is the psychological-safety signal.
3. **Write the rotation guide.** A one-page section explaining how the four categories rotate across 1:1s. Default rhythm: one prompt from a rotating category per weekly 1:1, so each IC sees one Category-1, one Category-2, and one Category-3 prompt per month, plus Category 4 at retros on a quarterly cadence. The rotation is a suggestion, not a mandate — the manager uses judgement about when a given IC needs a specific question.
4. **Add the "what not to do" list.** A half-page naming the common misuses (Chapter 7's failure-mode list, applied specifically to your prompts). Include: the atrophy audit, the performative question, the surveillance repurposing. If a manager follows the prompts but violates the "what not to do" list, the prompts do the opposite of their job.
5. **Write the Westrum team-level check.** A short (~1 paragraph) framing for retros that lets the manager assess, over the course of a quarter, whether the team is handling AI-wrong signal in a generative, bureaucratic, or pathological way. Three bullet points per Westrum type — the behavioural signs — plus the manager's intervention when they see bureaucratic or pathological signs (model the generative response in the next retro).
6. **Write the "hard conversation" note.** A one-paragraph note on how to navigate the settled-deference conversation in a 1:1 — separate behaviour from person, re-contextualise via the Chapter 6 ladder, keep the conversation inside the 1:1, do not escalate to HR on single-quarter signal. Point at Chapter 8 for the escalation ownership.
7. **Co-review with one senior IC and one peer manager.** Senior IC: *"if your manager asked you these questions, would you answer honestly? What would make you not answer honestly?"* The question tests psychological-safety assumptions baked into the phrasing. Peer manager: *"how would you adapt this to your team's rhythm?"* Record feedback and iterate.
8. **Soft-launch for one month.** Before adopting the prompts formally, run them in your own 1:1s for four weeks. Note which prompts felt artificial, which produced unexpectedly rich conversations, which got short answers that suggested the phrasing was off. Revise, then publish to the team.

## Starter guidance

- **Specificity beats earnestness.** *"Walk me through a time this week you accepted an AI suggestion without changing it"* is stronger than *"how do you feel about using AI."* The specificity invites a narrative; the narrative surfaces the behaviour.
- **One prompt per 1:1, not four.** The 1:1 is not about this module. The prompt is one thread in a broader conversation about the IC's work, growth, and context. Treat it that way.
- **Tone matters more than wording.** The same prompt can be asked curiously or auditingly. If the manager's affect is sharp, the IC's answer is performative. Practise the tone, not only the words.
- **Follow-through signals safety.** The single strongest trust-building move is returning to a prior week's conversation. *"Last week you weren't sure if the AI's suggestion on X was right — did you come back to it?"* Even if the follow-up produces nothing new, the fact of the return is signal.
- **Write the prompts in natural language.** *"Walk me through the time you..."* reads differently than *"Describe an instance in which..."* The first is conversational; the second is clinical. Pick conversational.
- **Do not combine prompts into a checklist.** The 1:1 is not an interview. If the prompts become a checklist, the IC feels interviewed, and the signal degrades.
- **Trust yourself to skip prompts when they do not fit.** The rotation is a default, not a schedule. An IC in the middle of a hard production issue does not need a Category 2 prompt that week; they need their manager's attention on the issue.
- **Retro prompts are harder than 1:1 prompts.** Team settings have a different psychological-safety floor than one-on-one settings. Retro prompts should be the ones most carefully tone-tested in co-review.
- **Stop using a prompt if it stops producing signal.** Six months of asking Category 1 Variant 2 and getting the same shape of answer every time means the prompt is stale. Rotate it out of the bank and add a new one.

## Acceptance criteria

The prompt bank is complete when:

- Each of the four categories has 4–6 prompt variants with the three annotation fields (when / what / how).
- A one-page rotation guide describes how the four categories cycle across 1:1s, and how Category 4 lands in retros.
- A half-page "what not to do" list is present, specifically naming the atrophy-audit, performative-question, and surveillance-repurposing failures.
- A short Westrum team-level check describes the three cultures' behavioural signs and the manager's intervention.
- A "hard conversation" note describes the settled-deference growth conversation, with the explicit boundary against single-quarter escalation to HR (pointer to Chapter 8).
- A four-week soft-launch log describes which prompts worked, which were revised, which were dropped.
- A change-log entry naming the senior-IC and peer-manager reviewers and the corrections.
- Length: ~2 printed pages for the prompt bank; the appendices (rotation guide, what-not-to-do, Westrum check, hard conversation) add another 1–2 pages. Total 3–4 pages.
- A manager running their first 1:1 next week could open the prompt bank and pick one prompt confidently.

## Stretch goals

- **A matched-set for retros.** Author a 4–6-prompt retro bank keyed to specific retro formats the team uses (e.g., start-stop-continue, four Ls, timeline retro). The AI-judgement prompt plugs into an existing section rather than becoming a new section.
- **A manager's self-assessment appendix.** Three or four prompts the manager asks *themselves* in their own weekly management-planning time: *"did I ask a Category-1 prompt this week on any IC on the outside-frontier task-type where it matters most?"* The self-assessment keeps the manager honest.
- **A psychological-safety baseline measurement.** Before launching the prompts, run Edmondson's seven-item psychological-safety team survey once anonymously, then again 6 months in. The delta is a soft measure of whether the prompts and the broader module interventions are strengthening or weakening safety.
- **Prompt phrasing calibration against the senior-IC co-review.** For the two or three prompts your senior-IC co-reviewer said they would not answer honestly, specifically redesign the phrasing. Save the before/after; the lessons compound.
- **Translate the Westrum check into a dashboard signal.** For managers running multiple teams or a manager-of-managers structure (mod-107 L50 territory), aggregate the retro-time Westrum observations into a 1-page rollup that predicts delivery risk. Note: this is at the edge of the single-team mod-107 scope and composes with the mod-109 incident work.
- **Compose with the hiring-loop work in mod-108.** Some of the deference-signal prompts can be adapted into interview-loop signals for new candidates. Author the adaptation; keep it coherent with mod-108's interview-redesign work.
