# Quiz 01 — Channels, Carve-Outs, Ladder, and the HR Boundary

**Format:** 12 multiple-choice questions.
**Coverage:** All eight chapters of mod-107.
**Scoring:** 1 point per question. 9/12 is a pass. Below that, re-read the chapter named in the answer explanation before moving on.

Each question tests whether you can apply the chapter, not whether you can quote it. Pick the single best answer.

---

## Question 1

A peer manager pushes back on the existence of this module: *"R10 posts at 0.08 — well below the 0.30 threshold this track applies to posting-based requirements. Why does mod-107 exist as a dedicated module at all?"* Per Chapter 1, the strongest response is:

- A. The 0.30 threshold is not strict; modules exist at any frequency the curriculum author chooses.
- B. The three research anchors — Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond; Noy & Zhang; Dell'Acqua et al. — ground three *distinct* channels of atrophy that compound below the quarterly output signal. The curriculum policy keeps R10 as a dedicated module because the empirical research grounds the practice regardless of current posting-frequency signal, and the frequency is expected to rise as the market codifies the risk.
- C. The posting language is miscoded; R10 is actually at 0.30+ in the sample.
- D. mod-107's content is bundled under mod-108; the standalone label is cosmetic.

## Question 2

Two quarters after AI-assistant rollout, your dashboards show DORA deployment frequency up, DX Core 4 self-reported satisfaction up, and PR throughput up ~25%. A peer manager cites the mid-year win and parks any further intervention. Per Chapter 1, the strongest critique of "parking it" is:

- A. The Year-1 numbers are statistical noise; discount the win entirely.
- B. The atrophy signal lags 12–36 months behind the policy decisions that produce it. The dashboard shows a win because the growth-path, differentiation, and deference channels compose below it — reviewers confirm output is correct without probing re-derivation; measured quality compresses; the deference pattern shapes a junior-IC growth curve the quarterly numbers cannot see. The managerial move is *not* to wait for the lagging indicator; it is to run the leading-indicator instrumentation (Chapter 7 1:1/retro prompts) and the acting-before-the-lagging-indicator intervention (Chapter 5 carve-outs) *now*.
- C. The right dashboard is DX Core 4, not DORA; swap them and the signal will surface.
- D. Delay the celebration to Year 2 and re-measure.

## Question 3

A direct report summarises Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond as *"AI provides a 14% productivity lift."* Per Chapter 2, the strongest critique of that summary is:

- A. The 14% is wrong; the study reports a different headline percentage.
- B. The 14% average is the least interesting finding for a manager. The load-bearing finding is the *distributional* pattern — gains concentrate at lower-tenure and lower-skilled agents — together with the *workforce-dynamics* channel (lower attrition, higher satisfaction, smaller promotion tenure gap). Citing only the average misses what mod-107 is built on. The responsible manager cite leads with the distributional pattern and the workforce-dynamics channel, names the growth-curve-shape concern the pattern raises, and applies the generalisation-from-customer-support caveat at the *percentage* level (the *pattern* generalises; the *number* does not).
- C. The study is a software-engineering study; cite the number directly for engineering teams.
- D. Inflate the number to the domain-specific extrapolation for your team.

## Question 4

A manager rewrites the Day-30 junior-IC check-in. Which rewrite is grounded in Brynjolfsson's Finding 2 (the distributional pattern)?

- A. Add a Cursor acceptance-rate dashboard review; flag anyone below 25%.
- B. Compare the IC's PR volume to the team median — above median = on track.
- C. Review the first solo-authored (AI-off) artifact *alongside* the first AI-assisted PR; ask the IC to narrate the two differently — what they could have done unassisted, what the AI changed about the artifact. Confirm the mentor pairing is working, confirm the IC has pushed back on an AI suggestion at least once in the prior two weeks, and name the next deliberate-practice carve-out on the calendar. Because AI carries the first-30-day productivity, the Day-30 instrument has to probe the growth channel the output itself no longer surfaces.
- D. Drop the Day-30 check-in — Brynjolfsson shows juniors need less supervision with AI.

## Question 5

A peer manager cites Noy & Zhang's *~40% time reduction* at a Q-review and recommends raising the Claude Code token cap by 50%. Per Chapter 3, the strongest critique is:

- A. The 40% is the correct justification for a token cap raise.
- B. The time-saving should be scaled up for engineering domains.
- C. The ~40% time reduction is the *average* finding, not the load-bearing finding for mod-107. The load-bearing finding is the *dispersion compression* — lower-skilled participants benefit more than higher-skilled participants on quality, and the top-to-bottom quality gap narrows. The compression means *promotion signal based on output volume and polish stops differentiating* — the ladder (Chapter 6) has to look at what still does. A token-cap raise is a mod-105 FinOps conversation, not a mod-107 finding; the peer manager is conflating the average lift with the dispersion compression, which Chapter 3's "common mistakes" list flags explicitly.
- D. Noy & Zhang's compression number directly justifies raising the cap.

## Question 6

A reviewer asks the Chapter 5 judgment question on an outside-frontier PR. The IC sincerely answers: *"Yes — I would have written it this way; I checked the AI's suggestion carefully, and I agree with it."* The artifact looks clean; the IC is honest. Per Chapter 4, the correct reviewer response is:

- A. The IC is a cyborg — approve the PR; judgment was applied.
- B. Accept the sincere endorsement as evidence judgment was applied; approve.
- C. The sincere endorsement is evidence that *review-the-AI's-output* was applied — it is **not** evidence that *re-derive-the-decision-from-scratch* was applied, and the two produce different outputs in exactly the cases Dell'Acqua et al. flag. The reviewer's response is to ask *"which parts of this did you actually produce yourself, and which parts did you accept?"* and then to look specifically at the accepted parts for whether the IC can defend them *without pointing at the AI's suggestion*. This is a cognitively costly re-derive-from-scratch probe; it is the uncritical-consumer-pattern intervention and must be rotated — Chapter 5 Carve-out 1 (paired-review rotation) and Carve-out 3 (judgment-weighted review pass) schedule it on outside-frontier task-types.
- D. The IC is the control condition; approve.

## Question 7

A senior IC proposes adding a fourth carve-out at the start of Q1 — a monthly deliberate-practice kata session for each IC. Per Chapter 5, the strongest response is:

- A. Accept — more carve-outs = more sustained judgment.
- B. Replace Carve-out 3 with the kata; kata practice is deeper deliberate practice.
- C. Thank the senior IC and hold at the three carve-outs for a full year. The three are the floor, not the ceiling — but the ceiling comes later. Teams that add more carve-outs at the start tend to lose them to the first production-pressure quarter and then lose faith in the entire anti-atrophy move. The right growth pattern is to run the three for a year, prove they stick and produce value, then layer on from a known-honest base. Chapter 7's retro mechanism is the way the team itself proposes the fourth carve-out, grounded in retro-surfaced signal — not the manager adding one in advance.
- D. Reject the proposal; adding carve-outs is not the manager's job.

## Question 8

In an end-of-quarter 1:1, an IC reports that they experienced the AI-off artifact (Carve-out 2) as *"a test"* rather than as growth. Per Chapter 5, this is:

- A. Appropriate — the AI-off artifact is a test of unassisted capability.
- B. A manager-side framing problem, listed in Chapter 5's common failure modes. The AI-off slot is a *comparison instrument*, not a test or a return to pre-AI practice. The right framing is *"what did you learn about what the AI is doing for you?"* — not *"let's see if you can still do this."* The review's purpose is to surface the comparison, not to grade the output. Fix: adjust the framing language in 1:1s and in the shared schedule, make the artifact visible as a team-wide practice (not an individual-performance signal), and ensure the artifact is pre-chosen, low-stakes, and scoped to 2–4 hours.
- C. A psychological-safety problem to escalate to HR.
- D. The IC's own interpretive problem; the artifact should stand as-is.

## Question 9

Per Chapter 6, which set of axes is the correct mod-107 ladder update?

- A. Prompt-engineering skill, token efficiency, AI tool fluency.
- B. Output volume, output polish, AI usage rate.
- C. Impact, scope, craft, leadership — the existing axes, unchanged.
- D. **Axis A — Judgement under AI augmentation**, **Axis B — Mentorship of judgement**, **Axis C — Review craft on AI-assisted work** — each scaled with level (junior = recognise, senior = routinely perform, staff = set team direction). The three axes map onto the three research anchors (Dell'Acqua / Noy & Zhang / Brynjolfsson). Levels and compensation bands stay as-is; the rubric at each rung changes shape. The amendment is additive, not a replacement — the existing descriptors stay in full, and three AI-augmentation bullets are added per tier.

## Question 10

A senior IC's measured output year-over-year is roughly flat under AI augmentation. A calibration-meeting peer argues the senior is stagnating and should be flagged. Per Chapter 6, the correct read is:

- A. The senior IC is stagnating; redirect their scope or exit them.
- B. Promote on tenure — flat output is acceptable at the senior tier.
- C. Under the updated ladder, ask whether Axis B (*mentorship of judgement*) has grown. If a junior IC who paired with the senior is now visibly running the judgement move independently, the senior's leverage has actually *increased* — it just lives in another IC's growth curve, which the old rubric did not surface. Noy & Zhang's compression finding predicts the senior's measured output will look flat; the updated ladder makes the senior's real leverage visible. The manager's calibration-meeting line is: *"compare on Axis A and Axis B, not on volume — volume compresses under AI, as Noy & Zhang (Science 2023) documents."*
- D. The flatness means the senior is under-using AI; coach them on prompts.

## Question 11

A peer manager argues that the annual engagement survey is the right instrument for detecting the atrophy signal on their team. Per Chapter 7, the strongest objection is:

- A. The survey is fine; just run it quarterly instead of annually.
- B. Rewrite the survey's questions to include AI-usage items.
- C. Stop running the survey entirely.
- D. The survey *lags* (annual cadence), *aggregates* (collapses the specific sentence the atrophy signal lives in), and *anonymises* (removes attribution needed for specific follow-up) — exactly the properties mod-107 cannot afford. The right instruments are the 1:1 (weekly) and the retro (per sprint / fortnightly), grounded in **Edmondson's psychological-safety construct** (candour lives in trusted relationships; deference is often *silent* and will not appear in an aggregated survey) and **Westrum's culture typology** (generative / bureaucratic / pathological handling of AI-wrong events shows up in the retro, not in surveys). Running the survey as the atrophy instrument is what Chapter 7 names *survey theatre*. The survey still has a role for aggregate-culture measurement across teams and years; it is the wrong instrument for *this* signal.

## Question 12

After two quarters of Chapter 5 carve-outs, Chapter 7 prompts, and Chapter 6 ladder conversations with a junior IC, the deference pattern has not shifted — the IC still cannot name a rejection of an AI suggestion, the second AI-off artifact was no closer to baseline than the first, and paired review surfaces the same gaps. Per Chapter 8, the correct manager move is:

- A. Open an informal performance improvement plan with weekly check-ins, written warnings, and performance-contingent consequences — call it a growth plan to avoid HR friction.
- B. Say in the next 1:1: *"If this pattern doesn't change by next quarter, we'll have to talk about your role."*
- C. Open the **Handoff-1** conversation with the HR partner — as a *question*, not a recommendation. Bring (i) two quarters of documented observations (specific 1:1 notes, retro content, Carve-out outcomes for this IC), (ii) a specific description of what has been tried and what the signal has been, and (iii) the open question *"is this a growth-conversation pattern I should continue to work on in 1:1s, or is this now a conversation we should structure differently?"* HR assesses with the manager whether a formal process is appropriate; opening one is one possible outcome, not the default — "keep working on it in 1:1s for another quarter, with a sharpened structure" is also a common outcome. The Chapter 7 prompts and Chapter 5 carve-outs are *not* repurposed as evidence for a formal process.
- D. Cite Dell'Acqua et al. in the HR conversation as justification that *this specific IC* is deferring; the research is the evidence.

## Answer key

1. **B.** Chapter 1 is explicit: R10's 0.08 posting frequency is below the 0.30 threshold, and the module exists anyway because the authoritative empirical research — Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond (NBER WP 31161); Noy & Zhang (Science 2023); Dell'Acqua et al. (HBS WP 24-013) — is "strong enough, published enough, and consistent enough across three independent studies that absence of this module would be a defect in a level-40 team-manager track regardless of what the current posting language says." The three findings describe three distinct channels that compound. (Chapter 1, "Motivation" and "The three channels of atrophy, named plainly.")

2. **B.** Chapter 1 names the signal-is-lagged argument as load-bearing: the failure mode is subtle (reviewer-confirmed output hides the absence of IC judgement), the output metrics look fine (Noy & Zhang's compression means the bottom quartile *improves* on measured output), and the feedback loop is slow (junior ICs grow through 18–24 months of carefully-exposed problem-solving — if the exposure is structurally different for the first 24 months, you will not see the gap until month 25). The managerial implication is *not* to wait for the signal — it is to instrument leading indicators (Chapter 7) and intervene before the lagging indicator arrives (Chapter 5). (Chapter 1, "Why the signal is lagged by 12–36 months.")

3. **B.** Chapter 2's "common mistakes when citing this paper" lists *"Citing the 14% number without the distributional context"* as the first mistake and *"Ignoring the workforce-dynamics finding"* as the fourth. The load-bearing finding for mod-107 is the distributional pattern plus the attrition / satisfaction / promotion-tenure channel. The customer-support-vs-engineering generalisation is defensible at the *pattern* level, not at the *percentage* level — Chapter 2 is explicit on citing the shape, not the number. (Chapter 2, "What the finding does *not* say" and "Common mistakes when citing this paper.")

4. **C.** Chapter 2's worked example is this exact Day-30 rewrite — the solo-authored-artifact comparison, the narration of what the IC could have done unassisted vs. what the AI changed, the push-back check, and the next deliberate-practice carve-out on the calendar. The rewrite is specifically grounded in Finding 2 (the distributional pattern): because the AI will carry the first-30-day productivity, the Day-30 instrument has to probe the growth channel that the output no longer surfaces. (Chapter 2, "Example: using Finding 2 to justify a change in Day-30 check-in.")

5. **C.** Chapter 3's "common mistakes" lists *"Citing the 40% time reduction as a direct engineering-productivity number"* and *"Using the quality lift to argue 'AI makes everyone better'"* as the two most common misreads. The load-bearing finding is the dispersion compression (Finding 3), not the average time-saving. The managerial implication is on the *promotion signal* (Chapter 6), not on token-cap decisions. The peer manager is conflating the *average lift* with the *dispersion compression* — two different findings with different managerial implications. (Chapter 3, "Why Finding 3 is the one that reshapes your ladder" and "Common mistakes when citing this paper.")

6. **C.** Chapter 4's "The hard case: the IC who sincerely believes the AI's output is correct" is this exact scenario. The sincere endorsement is evidence that *review-the-AI's-output* was applied, not that *re-derive-the-decision-from-scratch* was applied — and the two produce different outputs in exactly the outside-frontier cases. The reviewer's response is a specific re-derive-from-scratch probe ("which parts did you actually produce yourself, and which did you accept?"), not more diff-reading. The move is cognitively costly and must be rotated; Chapter 5's paired-review rotation and judgment-weighted review pass are the schedule. (Chapter 4, "The hard case" and the policy-move list.)

7. **C.** Chapter 5's "Why three and not twelve" is explicit: a long list of interventions is easy to write and impossible to sustain; the three carve-outs are the minimum set the research supports and that a team can actually run quarterly without displacing production work. "Teams that add more carve-outs at the start — morning deliberate-practice katas, book clubs, deep-dive learning Fridays — tend to lose them to the first production-pressure quarter and then lose faith in the entire anti-atrophy move." The right growth pattern is: run the three for a year, prove they stick, then layer on from a known-honest base; the retro mechanism (Chapter 7) is how the team itself proposes the fourth carve-out. (Chapter 5, "Why three and not twelve.")

8. **B.** Chapter 5's "Common failure modes when running the carve-outs" names *"ICs feel the AI-off slot is a test"* as a manager-side framing problem: *"It is not; it is a comparison instrument. The manager's language about it matters. Frame it as 'what did you learn about what the AI is doing for you?' not as 'let's see if you can still do this.'"* The fix is in the framing, the shared visibility of the schedule (not only in 1:1s — framing the carve-out as a team practice protects psychological safety per Chapter 7), and the artifact's scope (pre-chosen, low-stakes, 2–4 hours). This is a framing problem, not an HR problem. (Chapter 5, "Common failure modes when running the carve-outs.")

9. **D.** Chapter 6's "The three new axes, named" is explicit: Axis A — judgement under AI augmentation (grounded in Dell'Acqua); Axis B — mentorship of judgement (grounded in Noy & Zhang's differentiation finding, where senior-IC leverage lives in other ICs' growth curves); Axis C — review craft on AI-assisted work (grounded in Chapter 5's deference-channel intervention). Each is scaled with level. Compensation bands stay; the rubric changes shape. The amendment is additive — existing descriptors stay in full with three AI-augmentation bullets added per tier. Options A, B, and C all miss: A is tool-fluency language the chapter specifically names as under-weighted; B is the pre-AI-augmentation rubric; C removes the amendment. (Chapter 6, "What stays, what changes" and "The three new axes, named.")

10. **C.** Chapter 6's *Failure mode 2 — the senior IC who looks flat* is exactly this scenario. Under the old ladder a flat-output senior appears to be stagnating; under the updated ladder the question is whether their Axis B (mentorship of judgement) has grown — if a junior IC who paired with them is now visibly running the judgement move independently, the senior's leverage has *increased* and lives in another IC's growth curve. Noy & Zhang's compression finding predicts the top-tier output will look flat; the updated ladder makes the real leverage visible. The chapter also provides the calibration-meeting line: *"compare on Axis A and Axis B, not on volume — volume compresses under AI, as Noy & Zhang's work documents."* (Chapter 6, "How the ladder prevents three failure modes — Failure mode 2" and "Where this hits compensation calibration.")

11. **D.** Chapter 7's "Why the 1:1 and the retro, not the survey" gives four reasons: cadence (weekly / per-sprint vs. annual), attribution (1:1 is attributed by construction; the survey is anonymised), follow-through (the 1:1 permits a check-back next week), and candour (Edmondson's and Westrum/DORA research both find candour lives in trusted relationships, which is the 1:1, not an abstraction). Running the survey as the atrophy instrument is what the chapter names *survey theatre*. The survey has an aggregate-culture-measurement role; it is the wrong instrument for the specific-attributed-actionable signal mod-107 needs. (Chapter 7, "Why the 1:1 and the retro, not the survey.")

12. **C.** Chapter 8's "Handoff 1 — the 'I've tried the Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 interventions and the pattern hasn't shifted' conversation" is this exact scenario. The manager brings two quarters of documented observations, a specific description of what has been tried and what the signal has been, and a *specific open question* — *not* a pre-decided recommendation. HR's role is to assess *with* the manager whether a formal process is appropriate; opening one is one possible outcome, not the default. Option A is the "informal PIP" failure Chapter 8 names explicitly; B is the "threatening consequences" failure; D is the specific mis-cite the chapter flags — *"Do not cite Dell'Acqua or Noy & Zhang as justification in an HR conversation about an individual IC. The research is about populations; an individual's pattern is specific. The research is justification for the carve-outs and the ladder; it is not evidence that this particular IC is deferring."* (Chapter 8, "The three handoffs across the boundary — Handoff 1" and "What the manager must *not* do.")
