# Chapter 1 — The AI-Incident Problem and Why This Module Exists

> *The accountability contract pinned on the team's wiki in mod-103 Chapter 5 is a promise about the normal case — AI drafts, humans verify, shipping is on the humans. This module is about the day that promise is tested: the agent shipped something wrong, something broke downstream, someone is asking who owns the response. On that day, a slogan and three paragraphs are not enough. You need a runbook, a post-mortem discipline, a triage taxonomy, a routing contract with your specialist peers, and a cadence that folds the learning back into the standards. That is mod-109.*

## Motivation

[`mod-103` Chapter 5](../mod-103-review-standards-and-eval-commissioning/05-the-accountability-contract-ai-generates-humans-own.md) authored the team's **accountability contract** — three paragraphs plus a change log, pinned next to the review checklist, specifying who is on the hook when an AI-produced artifact ships broken. The contract names the four parties (IC, reviewer, manager, vendor), the C4 play ("what happens when AI is wrong"), and the commitment to blameless post-mortem practice. It was authored in the ordinary case, when nothing was actively on fire, so that when something is on fire the team is not improvising.

mod-109 is the practice that runs when the contract is invoked. Four changes under AI augmentation make that practice different enough from pre-AI incident response that it deserves its own module:

1. **The fault chain has layers that did not exist before.** A pre-AI incident chain ran *reviewer → IC → code*. An AI-produced incident chain runs *reviewer → IC → prompt → agent policy → tool authorisations → retrieved context → model behaviour → the artifact*. The [Google SRE](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) and [Allspaw](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems/) post-mortem disciplines hold, but the *fault chain* they walk now includes layers an SRE book from 2016 does not name.
2. **"The model did it" is the available dodge.** Every actor in the chain can push accountability sideways onto the agent, and the agent cannot push back. The post-mortem practice has to prevent this specifically — Chapter 3 is the adaptation. The rule is simple: the team cannot blame the model, but the team also cannot *excuse* the system by pointing at the model. Blame goes to roles and systems; the model is part of the system the roles own.
3. **The incident classes are new.** Hallucination-driven harm, prompt-injection exploitation, unauthorised-tool-use, and model regression are not variants of existing incident classes. They have their own triage questions, their own trace-preservation needs, and their own precedent base in the [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/) and the [OECD.AI AI Incidents Monitor](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents). Chapter 4 is the taxonomy.
4. **The specialist is a peer, not a subordinate.** Pre-AI, a tricky incident routed to a security engineer or a senior IC on the manager's team. Under AI augmentation, engineered-risk depth lives at [`ai-risk-engineer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/ai-risk-engineer-learning) — a peer-level (L25, AI Governance family) role in a different reporting line. SOC-side exposure routes to a security-operations peer in yet another line. The manager coordinates across two peer specialties they do not control, and keeps team-scope communication coherent. Chapter 5 is the routing contract.

The module is also where the manager prevents a recurring failure mode: the incident runs, the post-mortem is written, the write-up is filed — and nothing in the team's standards, evals, or routing matrix changes. Six weeks later the next incident of the same shape lands. Chapter 6 is the backfeed discipline that closes that loop; Exercise 05 is the ritual that makes it survive the quarter.

This chapter does three things. First, it names what is new and what is not about AI incidents at team scope. Second, it names the five pieces of mod-109 and how they compose. Third, it draws the scope boundary against the specialties around the manager — `ai-risk-engineer` sideways, SecOps sideways, counsel out-of-scope, L45+ upward.

## Core concepts

### What is not new

Three things are inherited and do not need re-authoring:

- **The blameless post-mortem discipline itself.** Google SRE's *Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure* and Allspaw's 2012 Etsy essay *Blameless Post-Mortems and a Just Culture* remain the methodological anchors. The rubric — "what did we expect, what happened, what did we learn, what are we changing" — is correct and applies to AI-produced failures unchanged. Chapter 3 extends it; it does not replace it.
- **Severity ladders and detect-triage-contain-eradicate-recover-learn.** The IR flow the team already runs (modelled on NIST SP 800-61 computer-security incident handling, Google SRE *Managing Incidents*, or whatever the organisation's own runbook uses) is the frame. Chapter 2 maps the C4 play onto whichever flow is already in place rather than introducing a competing one.
- **The accountability stack.** mod-103 Chapter 5's four parties — IC accountable for submission, reviewer for approval, manager for the system, vendor for the SLA — hold. This module operates them, it does not re-derive them.

### What is new, concretely

- **The trace that has to be preserved is larger.** The pre-AI reproduction artifact is code + inputs + logs. The AI-produced artifact adds the prompt (or prompt chain), the model and version, the agent harness and version, the tool / MCP authorisations the agent was allowed to call, the retrieved context the agent saw (RAG hits, file contents, tool-call returns), and the exact as-submitted artifact. Without those, the fault chain cannot be walked. Chapter 2 names the trace-preservation checklist; Exercise 01 produces it for the team.
- **The fault chain has to include the harness and the policy, not only the model.** Prompt-injection, unauthorised-tool-use, and model-regression incidents frequently have their root cause one or two layers away from the model output — in the agent policy (what the agent was allowed to do), in the harness (what the harness enforced), or in the tool connection (what the tool was authorised to call). A post-mortem that concludes "the model hallucinated" without inspecting those layers has stopped short.
- **Four AI-specific incident classes exist as first-class entities.** Hallucination-driven harm, prompt-injection exploitation, unauthorised-tool-use, and model regression have their own triage questions and their own precedents. The [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/) (run by the Responsible AI Collaborative) catalogues hundreds of real-world cases; the [OECD.AI AI Incidents Monitor](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents) is the complementary policy-maker-facing database. Chapter 4 reads both as sources; Exercise 03 is the rehearsal that teaches the team to read them without pattern-matching superficially.
- **Two peer specialists have genuinely different contracts.** `ai-risk-engineer` (L25, AI Governance family) owns the engineered root-cause chain — red-team reproduction, guardrail re-design, model-level evaluation. SecOps / DFIR owns the SOC-side chain when the incident touches data exposure, credential compromise, or intrusion. Pre-AI, security might have been one of the two; the engineered-risk specialty is new and the manager has to run coordination across both. Chapter 5 is the routing contract and Exercise 04 is the co-authored artifact.
- **The regression loop closes into two upstream artifacts, not one.** The post-mortem has to land both as an eval row in the mod-103 commissioning backlog (so a future regression is caught automatically) and as a routing-matrix or HITL-checkpoint amendment in the mod-106 delegation playbook (so a future workflow shape avoids the same failure). Chapter 6 is the backfeed; Exercise 05 is the ritual.

### What this module is *not*

- **Not a red-team or engineered-risk course.** Adversarial testing of models, guardrail design, systematic prompt-injection evaluation, and model-level red-teaming belong to [`ai-risk-engineer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/ai-risk-engineer-learning) (L25, AI Governance family) and, at frontier capability scope, to [`agentic-safety-engineer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/agentic-safety-engineer-learning) (L40, AI Governance family). The manager *commissions* engineered-risk work and *acts* on its outputs; they do not re-run it. Chapter 5 is explicit on the line.
- **Not a SOC / DFIR course.** When an incident involves data exposure, credential compromise, or intrusion, the SOC owns the response end-to-end — detection, forensic timeline, eradication, recovery, and regulator-facing timeline. The manager's team routes to SecOps and participates per the organisation's security IR process. Chapter 5 names the trigger line.
- **Not a legal-opinion course.** Regulator engagement (where notification obligations exist under the EU AI Act, GDPR breach notification, state breach-notification laws, or sector-specific regulators), public disclosure, and formal vendor-SLA invocation belong to counsel through the appropriate partner (General Counsel, Chief Privacy Officer, Chief Information Security Officer). The manager flags triggers; counsel decides. Chapter 7 names the triggers.
- **Not a cross-team or program-level incident course.** Multi-team incident coordination and public-facing post-mortem authoring belong to [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) (L45) and [`ai-engineering-manager`](https://github.com/ai-engineering-curriculum/ai-engineering-manager-learning) (L50). Program-level incident reporting and board-level communication belong to [`head-of-ai-governance`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/head-of-ai-governance-learning) (L60) and [`chief-ai-officer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/chief-ai-officer-learning) (L70). mod-109 covers *team-scope* incident response and names the upward handoffs.
- **Not a performance-management module.** When an incident's fault chain surfaces an IC-side pattern — repeated AI-deference, inadequate review, bypass of HITL checkpoints — the growth conversation is a mod-108 / mod-107 question, not an incident question. The blameless post-mortem does not become the performance review. Chapter 3 is explicit on the separation.

## Example: the day the slogan fails

A composite shaped example to make the scope concrete; calibration, not a case study.

A level-40 team manager inherits a team that has been running Claude Code and Cursor on an internal data-processing pipeline for two quarters. The accountability contract from `mod-103` Chapter 5 is pinned in the team's handbook. The team ships a change that passes the review checklist, is approved by the designated reviewer in under four minutes, and merges on a Thursday evening. The following Monday, a partner-facing report fed by that pipeline contains numbers that are plausibly wrong — rounded one decimal place too few, a column aggregation off by a factor the team recognises as a classic bias in the underlying join. Downstream, two executive dashboards pulled the number before anyone caught it. A VP asks the manager at 10:04 Monday morning: *"what happened, and who owns it?"*

The manager realises, in that moment, several things the accountability contract did not specify.

- **Where does the detection channel live?** The team's existing incident channel is for production outages, not for "a number looks wrong on a partner report." The report-side reviewer is not on the engineering team; they flagged it in a different Slack channel that nobody on the pipeline team monitors. Thirty-six hours have already passed.
- **What has to be preserved right now?** The Claude Code session that drafted the change is still on the IC's laptop, in a session the IC was about to close to free RAM. The exact prompt, the retrieved context the agent used, the sequence of file edits, and the specific model version are all still recoverable *right now* — but if the session closes, they are not. The manager has not previously named this as a preservation obligation; the IC has not been told.
- **Which specialist owns this?** The engineered-risk peer the organisation chartered three months ago has not been involved; the manager is not sure whether this reaches the bar for engineered-risk reproduction. The SOC has not been involved; there's no data-exposure signal. Legal has not been involved; there is no regulator in the picture. But the executive-dashboard number was shared externally in a partner update email over the weekend — a disclosure question is implicit.
- **What does the team learn, specifically?** The accountability contract says "we practise blameless post-mortem." The manager has read Allspaw and the Google SRE book. But the post-mortem template the team uses was written for production outages; it does not have fields for *prompt used*, *agent harness*, *retrieved context*, *tool authorisations*, *model version at the time*. The manager will write them in by hand this week and will likely forget half of them.
- **Where does this land back in the standards?** Even if the post-mortem is well-written, the manager does not yet have a ritual for turning a closed incident into a landed eval row in the mod-103 commissioning backlog. The incident write-up will sit in the shared drive; the next incident of the same shape will land in six weeks. Nothing structurally changes.

The manager runs the incident through a weekend of overtime, publishes a clean blameless write-up two weeks later, apologises to the executive stakeholder for the partner-report number, and gets credit for a professional response. But the team has learned nothing durable: the runbook does not exist, the trace-preservation is one engineer's memory, the engineered-risk peer was never consulted, and the standards-as-code stack has not changed.

That is the gap mod-109 is for. The five artifacts in this module are not a substitute for the manager's judgement during a live incident; they are the scaffolding that means the manager's judgement is spent on the *case-specific* decisions rather than on improvising the infrastructure for every incident response.

## Summary

- The accountability contract from [`mod-103` Chapter 5](../mod-103-review-standards-and-eval-commissioning/05-the-accountability-contract-ai-generates-humans-own.md) is a promise about the normal case; mod-109 is the practice that runs when the promise is tested.
- **What is not new:** blameless post-mortem discipline ([Google SRE](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/), [Allspaw](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems/)); severity ladders and detect-triage-contain-eradicate-recover-learn; the four-party accountability stack (IC, reviewer, manager, vendor).
- **What is new:** the trace to preserve is larger (prompt, agent harness, tool authorisations, retrieved context, model version); the fault chain has layers (harness, policy, tool connection) that are often the actual root cause; four AI-specific incident classes exist ([hallucination-driven harm, prompt-injection exploitation, unauthorised-tool-use, model regression](04-ai-specific-incident-taxonomy-and-triage.md)); two peer specialists (`ai-risk-engineer`, SecOps) run parallel chains the manager has to coordinate; the backfeed loop closes into both mod-103 evals *and* mod-106 routing.
- **Five artifacts over the module:** [team incident runbook](exercises/exercise-01-team-incident-runbook-authoring.md), [AI-adapted post-mortem template](exercises/exercise-02-blameless-postmortem-adapted-for-ai.md), [AIID / OECD.AI rehearsal](exercises/exercise-03-ai-incident-database-case-read-and-adapt.md), [routing contract with risk engineer and SecOps](exercises/exercise-04-routing-contract-with-risk-engineer-and-secops.md), [regression-fixture backfeed ritual](exercises/exercise-05-regression-fixture-backfeed-ritual.md).
- **Scope boundary:** not a red-team / engineered-risk course (sideways to [`ai-risk-engineer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/ai-risk-engineer-learning)); not a SOC / DFIR course (sideways to SecOps); not a legal-opinion course (out of scope to counsel); not a cross-team or program-level course (upward to L45+); not a performance-management module (links to [`mod-108`](../mod-108-hiring-performance-and-feedback-with-ai-in-the-loop/)).
- **Composes with** [`mod-101`](../mod-101-ai-augmented-team-manager-position-and-contract/) (contract C4), [`mod-103`](../mod-103-review-standards-and-eval-commissioning/) (standards and evals), [`mod-106`](../mod-106-workflow-redesign-and-agent-delegation/) (routing, HITL, tool-authorisation), [`mod-108`](../mod-108-hiring-performance-and-feedback-with-ai-in-the-loop/) (separation from performance management), [`mod-110`](../mod-110-ai-governance-and-policy-at-team-scope/) ([NIST AI RMF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf) MANAGE evidence), and [`mod-111`](../mod-111-vendor-budget-and-tool-selection-at-team-scope/) (vendor SLA in root-cause chain).
