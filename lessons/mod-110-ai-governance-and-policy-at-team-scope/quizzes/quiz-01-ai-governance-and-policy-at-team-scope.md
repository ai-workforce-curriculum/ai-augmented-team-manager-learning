# Quiz 01 — AI Governance, Policy, and Compliance at Team Scope

**Format:** 11 multiple-choice questions.
**Coverage:** All seven chapters of mod-110.
**Scoring:** 1 point per question. 8/11 is a pass. Below that, re-read the chapter named in the answer explanation before moving on.

Each question tests whether you can apply the chapter to a specific scope-of-practice decision the manager actually faces at team scope, not whether you can quote it. Pick the single best answer.

---

## Question 1

A peer manager reads Chapter 1 and pushes back: *"If the head of AI governance owns the program, the architect owns policy structure, and counsel owns legal position, what is actually left for a level-40 manager to do?"* The strongest response is:

- A. The manager writes the organisation's AI policy because no one else is close enough to the work; counsel and the architect sign off on the manager's draft.
- B. **The manager owns *practice* — not policy, not program accountability, not legal position.** Chapter 1 draws the shorthand explicitly: *"the manager owns practice, the architect owns policy structure, the head of governance owns program accountability, counsel owns legal position. Practice is runnable, logged, and evidence-producing."* The L40 manager is *"the smallest unit of real AI-governance ownership"* — the person who signs off on what their ICs put into which agent, schedules the Article 4 literacy training, runs the Article 14 human-oversight checkpoint, and produces the quarterly evidence packet upward audits will actually read. The manager does not author policy; the manager *operates* policy and *evidences* operation — which is a full scope of work in its own right, not a residual.
- C. The manager is a pass-through — forward the policy, forward the training content, forward the audit questions; the specialists do the work.
- D. The manager only acts during incidents; steady-state governance lives entirely upstream.

## Question 2

A manager has drafted the team's NIST AI RMF GOVERN crosswalk. For GOVERN 2.1 (roles, responsibilities, lines of communication) the cell reads *"yes — the team has an accountability contract and a routing contract; see the team wiki."* Per Chapter 2, this cell is:

- A. Correct and complete; the audit will accept the team's self-attestation.
- B. **Not yet evidence.** Chapter 2 is explicit: *"the specific file path, wiki link, or repository location where the evidence lives. Not 'Slack'; not 'the team wiki'; the specific URL. If the evidence does not exist, the cell is a gap marker with owner and date."* "The team wiki" is a wave at the artifact, not a citation. A cell needs a specific URI (e.g., `https://wiki.acme/ai/accountability-contract-v2.3`) plus — per the Playbook's *Transparency and documentation* discipline Chapter 2 names as the acceptance criterion — the artifact must actually answer the subcategory's documentation questions (*"who is accountable for the AI system? what is the escalation path if the system behaves unexpectedly?"*). The fix is to replace "see the team wiki" with the specific link for the mod-103 Ch. 5 accountability contract and the mod-109 Ch. 5 routing contract, and to verify both answer the Playbook's transparency questions for GOVERN 2.1.
- C. Correct for v0.1; cells can remain unspecific until v1.0.
- D. Incorrect only because the crosswalk should quote NIST verbatim; the paraphrase is the problem.

## Question 3

The manager is filling GOVERN 1.3 (communication of risk-management processes and outcomes). The organisation has not set an acceptable-risk threshold for any class of AI system; the team has been operating against implicit judgement. Per Chapter 2, the manager should:

- A. Invent a team-scope risk threshold so the crosswalk cell can be filled; document it as team policy.
- B. Mark the row "n/a" since no threshold exists to evidence against.
- C. **Fill the backing-team-artifact column with whatever the team does evidence (eval backlog, retrospective records, use-case register), leave the backing-upstream-artifact column empty, and open a gap-register row routed *upward* to `senior-ai-governance-architect` / `head-of-ai-governance`.** Chapter 2 names this silence directly: *"Risk thresholds are not in the framework. NIST does not say what constitutes 'acceptable' AI risk; it says the organisation must set and justify thresholds. At team scope, the manager inherits the organisation's thresholds; if none exist, the crosswalk routes GOVERN 1.3 upward as a gap."* Inventing a team-grain threshold is a scope violation (policy belongs upstream); marking the row n/a hides the gap the governance program most needs to see. The gap register is specifically the artifact for cases like this — owner (role), due date, routing (upward), notes.
- D. Use the threshold the vendor publishes in its model card as the team's threshold.

## Question 4

The firm's program-level AI-literacy curriculum covers Modules A, C, E, F, and G (foundations; regulatory landscape; data/IP; incident recognition; affected-person considerations). The manager plans to roll the curriculum out to the team verbatim. Per Chapter 3, the Article 4 evidence packet is still not complete. Why, and what is missing?

- A. Nothing is missing; inheriting the program-level curriculum fully discharges the deployer's Article 4 obligation.
- B. **Two of the seven baseline modules are team-specific and cannot be inherited.** Chapter 3 names them: **Module B — the team's specific AI stack** (which tools, which models, what data goes into each, what guardrails apply, where output lands — *"this is the module that cannot be inherited from a generic curriculum; it is team-specific"*) and **Module D — accountability, review, and the human-in-the-loop** (the mod-103 accountability contract, the mod-103 review checklist, the mod-106 delegation ladder and HITL design — *"the team's existing mod-103 and mod-106 material"*). Module B is the one the manager must author from scratch; Module D is inherited from the team's other artifacts but has to be present in the curriculum for the reviewer and manager tiers. A rollout of only A/C/E/F/G leaves ICs trained on generic AI foundations and the regulatory landscape but unable to point at *their own stack* or *their own accountability contract* when asked. The evidence shape also fails the audit's "sufficient to what the person does" test — Article 4's own language.
- C. Only Module B is missing; Module D is covered implicitly by on-the-job experience.
- D. The curriculum is complete but the sign-off from HR is missing.

## Question 5

The manager summarises the team's Article 4 evidence as: *"We did a lunch-and-learn in July, an AI-tool-rollout session in August, and an incident-retrospective debrief in September that doubled as training. Everyone on the team was at each session."* Per Chapter 3, this record is:

- A. Sufficient — attendance at three separate sessions in one quarter is strong evidence.
- B. Sufficient for the operational tier; the manager tier may need more depth.
- C. **Not audit-ready evidence.** Chapter 3 names this exact failure mode: *"we did a lunch-and-learn in July and everyone was there. Without a dated attendance record tied to a named curriculum module, this is not evidence."* The Article 4 evidence packet must answer five specific questions on demand — *who is on the team by role tier; what modules did each person complete with date; what is the current curriculum version with change-log; who signed off on the curriculum and when; when is each person's next refresh due and what triggers it?* A set of untagged sessions with no LMS record (or dated attendance log tied to a named module), no curriculum version, no per-person completion signal, and no refresh cadence answers none of the five. The fix is to retro-attribute each session to a named curriculum module, log dated attendance for each (even if reconstructed from calendar invites), and move forward with LMS enrolment per the role-tier segmentation table. "Off-LMS learning counts but only if logged" (per Chapter 3 and Exercise 02) is the rule to internalise.
- D. Audit-ready only if the manager personally vouches for attendance.

## Question 6

Counsel has classified the team's underwriting-assist workflow as *adjacent-to-high-risk under Annex III category 5(b)*. The manager begins drafting the Article 14 workflow sheet by inventing an oversight-role description, a new HITL checkpoint structure, a new override-mechanism design, and a new trace-preservation policy. Per Chapter 4, this approach is:

- A. Correct — Article 14 needs bespoke artifacts for every high-risk or adjacent workflow.
- B. Correct only if counsel has approved the bespoke designs.
- C. Correct if the team's prior artifacts are too generic to serve regulated workflows.
- D. **Incorrect — the Article 14 sheet is a composition artifact, not a new design.** Chapter 4 is explicit: *"the sheet is a mapping of existing artifacts to Article 14 language. If any field cannot be filled from existing artifacts, that is a gap in the team's stack and the exercise flags it."* The specific compositions Chapter 4 names: **the named oversight role** inherits from [`mod-103` Ch. 5](../../mod-103-review-standards-and-eval-commissioning/05-the-accountability-contract-ai-generates-humans-own.md) accountability contract; **the decision points** inherit from [`mod-106`](../../mod-106-workflow-redesign-and-agent-delegation/) HITL checkpoint design and delegation ladder; **the override mechanism** inherits from the mod-106 tool-authorisation policy and mod-103 review-checklist stop criteria; **the automation-bias mitigation** inherits from the mod-103 review checklist, the Chapter 3 Article 4 training, and [`mod-107`](../../mod-107-skill-growth-anti-atrophy-and-career-development/) anti-atrophy practice; **the trace preservation** inherits from the [`mod-109`](../../mod-109-accountability-incident-response-and-blameless-postmortems/) trace-preservation checklist; **the escalation path** inherits from the mod-109 routing contract. Where a field cannot be filled from existing artifacts, that is a gap in the team's stack — the sheet surfaces it, not papers over it.

## Question 7

A customer emails the team's generic support alias asking *"Is the reply I got yesterday generated by AI?"* The team's product has an agent that drafts first-pass replies that a human support agent reviews before sending; the organisation has not previously answered a question of this shape. Per Chapter 4, the manager's correct move is:

- A. Reply directly; the honest answer is "a human reviewed it" and that discharges any Article 50 obligation.
- B. Reply with a terse "no" since a human reviewed the output before it was sent.
- C. **Route to counsel; do not improvise the response.** Chapter 4's trigger list is explicit: *"The team receives a request from an affected person that implicates Article 50 (e.g., 'is this generated by AI?'). Counsel decides the response."* Article 50(1) obligations around AI interaction with natural persons, 50(4) disclosures around AI-generated text to inform the public, exemption pathways (human-reviewed editorial content may be exempt from some obligations under conditions), and the organisation's prior public commitments all matter to how the response is phrased — and all are legal-position questions. Chapter 4 also names *"any regulator-facing communication or request. The manager does not respond to regulators directly; counsel does."* The same discipline applies to affected-person communication implicating a statutory obligation. The manager flags, preserves the exchange in the trace, and opens a trigger-list entry; counsel decides the language and the channel. The team's Article 50 interaction sheet (if one applies) is then updated with counsel's approved disclosure text so the next time a similar question arrives, the sheet answers it.
- D. Ignore the email; Article 50 applies only to live-chat interfaces.

## Question 8

A VP suggests the team restructure its governance practice around Microsoft Responsible AI Standard v2 language — *"our enterprise customers all ask about it; let's make Microsoft RAI goals our primary framework and derive everything else from them."* Per Chapter 5, the strongest response is:

- A. Agree — customer questionnaires are the forcing function; Microsoft RAI is as good a framework as any.
- B. Agree in part — restructure the review checklist and the accountability contract to use Microsoft RAI language; leave the other artifacts alone.
- C. Decline — industry frameworks like Microsoft RAI are irrelevant to team practice; only NIST and the EU AI Act matter.
- D. **Decline *restructuring*; propose a defensive *mapping* table instead.** Chapter 5 names the discipline explicitly: *"the temptation with industry frameworks is to restructure the team's practice around their language. This is almost always a mistake at team altitude. The organisation's primary regulatory drivers are NIST RMF and the EU AI Act (plus sector-specific statutes). Reshaping the team around Microsoft RAI language competes with that hierarchy. Industry frameworks evolve — Google updated its principles repeatedly; Microsoft's Standard was revised from v1 to v2. Practice tied tightly to a specific version depreciates."* The defensive discipline is a one-to-two-page mapping artifact per framework that *points at* existing team artifacts under framework-appropriate principle names and is **re-pointed, not re-authored**, when the framework updates, the team artifact changes, or a new workflow enters scope. The audit — typically a customer security questionnaire or an internal commitment review — expects the organisation to be able to connect the public commitment to real practice *somewhere*; the mapping is that connector. The underlying NIST crosswalk, Article 4 program, mod-103 review stack, and mod-109 incident practice stay primary.

## Question 9

An IC pings the manager in Slack on a Tuesday afternoon: *"Our Copilot contract is zero-retention Enterprise tier, right? So I should be fine pasting this restricted-class customer file into it to debug a parsing branch — the vendor can't train on it or keep it."* Per Chapter 6, the manager's correct response is:

- A. Agree — zero-retention Enterprise is the contract tier that permits restricted data; proceed.
- B. Agree conditionally — have the IC email a confirmation to procurement, then proceed.
- C. **Correct the IC: zero-retention does not override the data-class × tool-class matrix.** Chapter 6 is explicit: *"ICs frequently equate 'enterprise contract' with 'anything goes'. The policy explicitly disabuses this."* Zero-retention is a *vendor-contract property* (the vendor commits not to retain or train on inputs); the data-class decision is a *team-policy property* (what classes of data may enter what tool class under what conditions, with vendor-clause citations). Chapter 6's matrix explicitly distinguishes both axes — the Restricted row's cell under *Enterprise coding assistant with zero-retention contract* is typically **prohibited** in the Chapter 6 illustration precisely to prevent this confusion. The matrix also distinguishes *"still allowed, allowed after transformation, prohibited"* — the right path is usually to generate a synthetic fixture that reproduces the branching behaviour (Internal class; permitted) or, where the specific real record is genuinely irreproducible synthetically, to open a tool-authorisation-policy exception per mod-106 that routes to the manager for sign-off with a specific justification. Chapter 6's whole point is that the IC should answer the question in 30 seconds by reading the matrix, not by reasoning about vendor contracts in Slack.
- D. Defer to the IC — the IC has contract-reader access; the manager should not second-guess.

## Question 10

A new manager inherits a team's draft data policy. The matrix has three cells reading "conditional — approved on a case-by-case basis by the manager." Per Chapter 6, these cells are:

- A. Correct — conditional cells route to the manager for judgement.
- B. Correct with a tightening: the manager should keep a written log of each case-by-case decision.
- C. **Effectively prohibited.** Chapter 6's design rule is explicit: *"'Conditional' is not 'ask your manager.' Each conditional cell names the specific condition — the vendor clause, the tagging requirement, the approval workflow, the audit trail. If the condition is not written down, the cell is effectively prohibited: no IC can take a conditional action safely without ambiguity."* A case-by-case cell shifts the decision into the manager's head, which (a) is not a policy, (b) cannot be audited, (c) does not scale beyond the current manager, and (d) leaks the C5 "what AI may see" clause of the human/machine contract back into ad-hoc interpretation — the exact failure the matrix exists to prevent. The fix is to rewrite each conditional cell to name the specific condition — e.g., *"Confidential × Enterprise coding assistant with zero-retention: conditional on per-document tagging per the tagging workflow at {URL}; vendor clause {citation} supports; approval not required for tagged documents, manager sign-off required for untagged exception requests per mod-106 tool-authorisation policy."* The matrix "is the policy, not the beginning of the policy."
- D. Correct provided the manager documents the policy in Slack whenever a case arises.

## Question 11

In the Q1 upward packet's gap register, the manager has a row reading: *"Workflow X plausibly within Annex III (employment-screening-adjacent); counsel is backlogged with a two-week turnaround. Owner: manager. Due date: next week. Routing: team-backlog. Notes: manager will draft an interim classification so the pilot can proceed."* Per Chapter 7, this gap-register row is:

- A. Correct — the manager owns the gap and can draft an interim classification to unblock the pilot.
- B. Correct for v0.1 — the gap register's point is to have owners; the specific routing is secondary.
- C. **Incorrect in two load-bearing ways Chapter 7 names explicitly.** First, **the owner and routing are wrong.** Chapter 7's ownership-boundary table is unambiguous: *"The manager is not a lawyer. Interpretation of statute, formal legal opinion, and regulator communication are not the manager's job — not even 'a little bit'. The triggers in Chapter 4 and Chapter 6 route to counsel, who decides."* An Annex III classification question is a legal-position question; the owner is counsel, the routing is *upward* (or sideways through GC per the firm's partner structure), and the manager's role is to flag the trigger and preserve evidence of the flag. Second, **drafting an interim classification is a scope violation** — the manager would be practising law without authority, and the pilot that proceeds on the interim would be running with an unapproved classification that an audit would later flag as a self-serving workaround. The right shape of the row is: *"Workflow X plausibly within Annex III (employment-screening-adjacent); classification required before pilot can start; owner: counsel (routed through GC); due date: {counsel's commitment}; routing: upward-legal; notes: trigger flagged {date}; pilot blocked pending classification; interim scope confirmed with product partner."* If counsel's backlog is the real problem, that is a separate gap routed to `head-of-ai-governance` on the staffing side — not a gap the manager fills by doing counsel's job.
- D. Incorrect only because the due date is too short.

## Answer key

1. **B.** Chapter 1's *"What governance means at team scope"* and *"Scope of decision / evidence / liability"* make the shorthand explicit: the manager owns practice; the architect owns policy structure; the head of governance owns program accountability; counsel owns legal position. The L40 role is named *"the smallest unit of real AI-governance ownership"*. (Chapter 1, *"What governance means at team scope"* and *"What this module is not"*.)

2. **B.** Chapter 2's *"The crosswalk artifact"* is explicit: *"Not 'Slack'; not 'the team wiki'; the specific URL."* The Playbook's *Transparency and documentation* questions are named as the acceptance criteria — *"If the team cannot answer them, the cell is a gap."* A cell with a generic "yes" fails both tests. (Chapter 2, *"The crosswalk artifact"* and *"Using the Playbook, not just the framework"*.)

3. **C.** Chapter 2's *"Where NIST intentionally does not prescribe"* names the two silences — risk thresholds and tooling — and explicitly routes the threshold case *"upward as a gap"*. Inventing a team-scope threshold is a policy-authoring move the manager does not own; "n/a" hides the gap the governance program most needs to see. The gap register with owner, due date, and upward routing is the shape. (Chapter 2, *"Where NIST intentionally does not prescribe"* and *"The crosswalk artifact"*.)

4. **B.** Chapter 3's *"Content coverage, in more detail"* names seven modules (A–G) and identifies **Module B — the team's specific AI stack** as the module the manager must author (*"this is the module that cannot be inherited from a generic curriculum; it is team-specific"*) and **Module D — accountability, review, and the human-in-the-loop** as the module inherited from the team's existing mod-103 and mod-106 material. A/C/E/F/G routinely inherit from the program-level curriculum. Shipping only A/C/E/F/G leaves ICs unable to point at their own stack or their own accountability contract. (Chapter 3, *"Content coverage, in more detail"* and *"The six dimensions of a team AI-literacy program"*.)

5. **C.** Chapter 3's *"Evidence capture: what the record has to show"* names the five audit questions (who, what, curriculum version, sign-off, next refresh) and the specific anti-pattern: *"we did a lunch-and-learn in July and everyone was there. Without a dated attendance record tied to a named curriculum module, this is not evidence."* The pattern that survives: *"LMS curriculum #AI-FLUENCY-TM-V2, person-level completion report attached (CSV), covering Modules A/B/C/D/E/F per segmentation table attached, average completion date 2026-10-15, next refresh 2027-01-15."* Off-LMS learning counts but only if logged with the five fields. (Chapter 3, *"Evidence capture: what the record has to show"*.)

6. **D.** Chapter 4's *"Composition with the rest of the track"* is explicit: *"the sheet is a mapping of existing artifacts to Article 14 language. If any field cannot be filled from existing artifacts, that is a gap in the team's stack and the exercise flags it."* The six composition lines (oversight role from mod-103 Ch. 5; decision points from mod-106; override mechanism from mod-106 + mod-103; automation-bias mitigation from mod-103 + Chapter 3 + mod-107; trace from mod-109; escalation from mod-109) are named one by one. A from-scratch draft both duplicates existing artifacts and produces a sheet the audit cannot trace back to the team's actual operating practice. (Chapter 4, *"The Article 14 workflow sheet"* and *"Composition with the rest of the track"*.)

7. **C.** Chapter 4's *"The triggers for routing to counsel"* lists five triggers; the fourth is verbatim: *"The team receives a request from an affected person that implicates Article 50 (e.g., 'is this generated by AI?'). Counsel decides the response."* The manager flags, preserves the exchange in the trace, and opens a trigger-list entry; counsel sets the language and the approved disclosure. Once counsel approves, the response is captured in the team's Article 50 interaction sheet so the next identical question is answered consistently. (Chapter 4, *"The triggers for routing to counsel"* and *"The Article 50 interaction sheet"*.)

8. **D.** Chapter 5's *"The defensive-mapping discipline"* names the three reasons restructuring is almost always wrong (NIST / AI Act are the primary drivers; frameworks evolve and version-tying depreciates; the substrate is what the organisation actually needs). *"The team's practice does not change to fit the table; the table changes to point at the practice."* The mapping is **re-pointed, not re-authored**, on three triggers (framework update, team artifact change, new workflow in scope). The audit that reads the mapping is typically a customer questionnaire, not the primary regulator audit — which Chapter 5's worked example (*"a partner security questionnaire"*) illustrates. (Chapter 5, *"The defensive-mapping discipline"*, *"Composing the two mappings"*, and *"What this chapter deliberately does not do"*.)

9. **C.** Chapter 6's *"What leaves the premise"* and the matrix illustration are both explicit: zero-retention is a vendor-contract property; the data-class decision is a team-policy decision; the two axes are independent. The matrix cell under (Restricted × Enterprise coding assistant with zero-retention) is **prohibited** in the Chapter 6 illustration. The fix path — synthetic fixture (Internal; permitted) or a mod-106 tool-authorisation-policy exception with manager sign-off — is the one the Chapter 6 worked example on the Tuesday-afternoon IC decision walks through. *"ICs frequently equate 'enterprise contract' with 'anything goes'. The policy explicitly disabuses this."* (Chapter 6, *"The data-class × tool-class matrix"*, *"What leaves the premise"*, and the Tuesday-afternoon example.)

10. **C.** Chapter 6's design rule is explicit: *"'Conditional' is not 'ask your manager.' Each conditional cell names the specific condition — the vendor clause, the tagging requirement, the approval workflow, the audit trail. If the condition is not written down, the cell is effectively prohibited."* A case-by-case cell is unaudited, unscalable, and manager-specific — all failure modes the matrix exists to eliminate. The fix is to name the specific condition (vendor clause citation, tagging workflow URL, approval path) so the IC can read the matrix and act without ambiguity. (Chapter 6, *"The data-class × tool-class matrix"*.)

11. **C.** Chapter 7's ownership-boundary table and the two closing lines are explicit: *"The manager is not a lawyer. Interpretation of statute, formal legal opinion, and regulator communication are not the manager's job — not even 'a little bit'. The triggers in Chapter 4 and Chapter 6 route to counsel, who decides."* An Annex III classification question is the archetype of the counsel-owned trigger. The correct gap-register row names the trigger, assigns counsel as owner, routes upward-legal, and blocks the pilot pending classification. If counsel's backlog is the real bottleneck, that is a separate staffing gap routed to `head-of-ai-governance` — not a gap the manager fills by drafting an interim classification. *"A gap register with routing and owners is a plan"*; a gap register with the manager owning counsel's work is a scope violation. (Chapter 7, *"The ownership boundary, precisely"*, *"Structuring the gap register"*, and *"What the module does not produce"*.)
