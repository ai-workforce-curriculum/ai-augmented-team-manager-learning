# Chapter 1 — The Vendor-Selection Problem at Team Scope and Why This Module Exists

> *The tool registry is not what you have licences for. The tool registry is the dated, justified, revocable set of tools you can stand up in front of a security review on Tuesday and defend — with a build-vs-buy rationale, a data-handling clause, and a sunset condition for each one.*

## Motivation

The AI coding and knowledge-work tooling market is moving on a cadence shorter than most teams' procurement cycles. Between early 2024 and late 2026, at least eight serious coding copilots — [Cursor](https://www.cursor.com/), [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot), [Claude Code](https://docs.claude.com/en/docs/claude-code), [Windsurf](https://windsurf.com/), [Continue](https://docs.continue.dev/), [Sourcegraph Cody](https://sourcegraph.com/docs/cody), [Zed](https://zed.dev/docs/ai), [JetBrains AI Assistant](https://www.jetbrains.com/ai/) — and four serious agent frameworks — [LangChain / LangGraph](https://langchain-ai.github.io/langgraph/), [CrewAI](https://docs.crewai.com/), [AutoGen](https://microsoft.github.io/autogen/), [PydanticAI](https://ai.pydantic.dev/) — reached production-grade adoption. Each is positioned differently: some ship as IDE forks, some as plugins, some as terminal-native agents, some as frameworks for the team to assemble its own. The default behaviour of a level-40 manager under that pressure is one of three failure modes:

- **Default-tool drift.** The team uses whatever each IC installed first. The budget is a trailing sum of consumer-tier charges; the data posture is undefined; the question "which model saw our codebase this quarter" has no answer.
- **Early-lock enterprise commit.** The manager signs a multi-year commitment with one vendor on the basis of a six-week bake-off conducted when the market looked different. Twelve months later, the team is paying for seats on a product the market has moved past, with contractual exit friction.
- **Governance theatre.** The team has a tool-authorisation policy (from [`mod-106`](../mod-106-workflow-redesign-and-agent-delegation/)) that lists tools in principle but has no evaluation rationale, no cost model, and no sunset discipline. The policy is a paragraph; the registry is the artifact an audit actually reads.

mod-111 is the module that replaces all three failure modes with a single artifact: a **tool registry**, maintained at team scope, in which each tool has a dated evaluation rationale (Chapter 2), a build-vs-buy position (Chapter 3), a budget allocation aligned with [`mod-105`](../mod-105-team-ai-enablement-programs/) stage ladder (Chapter 5), a due-diligence record (Chapter 6), and an exit condition (Chapter 7). The registry is produced by composing the five exercises; it is the composed output the module delivers.

This chapter does three things. First, it names what *team-scope vendor selection* means and what it does not. Second, it names the five pieces of mod-111 and what they compose into. Third, it draws the scope boundary against the specialties around the manager: enterprise procurement upward to [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning), vendor-security-assessment sideways to SecOps, long-term commit architecture sideways to FinOps, clause interpretation out of scope to legal / privacy office.

## Core concepts

### What vendor selection means at team scope

Three practical differences shape every artifact in this module.

- **Scope of decision.** The manager does not sign the organisation's master agreement with a vendor. The manager picks *which* tools the team uses *within* the organisation's existing agreements, *which* tier (enterprise vs business vs consumer) the team runs on when multiple exist, and *which* new tool to stand up a trial for when demand emerges from the roadmap or the ritual calendar. The manager's decisions are the ones *inside* the enterprise framework's envelope: *which* and *how* and *when to sunset*, not *whether the organisation does business with the vendor at all*.
- **Scope of evidence.** The manager produces team-scope evidence: the evaluation rationale per tool, the budget allocation per IC, the due-diligence record per vendor, the exit plan per entry on the registry. The evidence must be structured so that an org-wide procurement rollup (owned by [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning)) and an org-wide governance rollup (owned by `senior-ai-governance-architect` and above) can consume it without re-interviewing the manager. The registry format is deliberately shaped for both consumers.
- **Scope of liability.** The manager is not the contractual counterparty with the vendor; the organisation is. The manager is the operational owner of the subset of the vendor contract that lives inside the team — the opt-outs configured, the data classes routed into the tool, the usage within the contracted ceiling. The manager flags questions that touch contract renegotiation, security posture change, or legal position change; procurement, SecOps, and legal decide them.

A useful shorthand: the manager owns **selection and evaluation**; procurement owns **negotiation and signature**; SecOps owns **security assessment depth**; legal owns **clause interpretation**; FinOps owns **commitment architecture**; [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) owns **the org-wide tool framework**. Selection is runnable, logged, and dated. The others are the upstream positions the selection decision operates against.

### What this module is *not*

- **Not a procurement course.** Enterprise-framework negotiation, multi-year committed-use architecture, master service agreement structuring, and the organisation's position on indemnity, liability caps, and IP warranties belong to procurement and [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) at L45. The manager is a *consumer* of the framework and a *contributor* of team-scope demand signal, pilot evidence, and renewal input to it.
- **Not a vendor security-assessment course.** Attack-surface analysis, penetration testing, red-teaming of the vendor's deployment, and deep architectural security review belong to SecOps. The manager fills the due-diligence checklist in Chapter 6 with answers that come *from* SecOps and the vendor's trust centre; the manager does not re-perform the assessment.
- **Not a FinOps course.** GPU reservation strategy, long-term committed-spend architecture, multi-cloud hosting-cost arbitrage, and enterprise rate-card negotiation belong to FinOps. The budget work in Chapter 5 is seat and token distribution *within* the budget the organisation has already committed; it is not the architecture of the commitment itself.
- **Not a legal-opinion course.** The formal interpretation of whether the vendor's DPA is adequate under GDPR Article 28, whether the SOC 2 scope covers the service variant the team uses, or whether the model-provenance clause meets EU AI Act Article 10 for a given use case belongs to counsel through the appropriate partner (General Counsel, Chief Privacy Officer, Data Protection Officer). The manager captures the clause state, records sign-off, and routes questions; counsel decides the legal position.
- **Not a capacity-planning or inference-infrastructure course.** Whether the team *self-hosts* an open-weight model to reduce vendor dependency is a decision that leans on `ai-infra-engineer` and `model-platform-engineer` specialities. mod-111 covers the manager's half of the conversation — demand signal, data-class fit, team-scope cost — not the infrastructure build.

### The live-market shape, briefly

A few things are true about the tooling market that shape this module's posture and that will probably still be true two cadences from the publication date, even if specific products shift:

- **The copilot market is unconsolidated.** No single tool has dominant market share in all three tiers — IDE plugin, terminal-native agent, agent-authoring environment. A team with one tool is almost always leaving leverage on the table on at least one tier.
- **Multiple serious open-source agent frameworks exist.** [LangChain / LangGraph](https://langchain-ai.github.io/langgraph/), [AutoGen](https://microsoft.github.io/autogen/), [CrewAI](https://docs.crewai.com/), [PydanticAI](https://ai.pydantic.dev/) each have defensible positioning, large communities, and real production users. Choosing one is a *stance*, not an obvious default.
- **Enterprise-tier contracts materially change the data posture.** [Copilot Business / Enterprise](https://resources.github.com/copilot-trust-center/), [Claude for Work / Enterprise](https://www.anthropic.com/enterprise), [Cursor Business](https://cursor.com/pricing), [OpenAI Enterprise](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/) each have contractual zero-retention or bounded-retention clauses distinct from the consumer or default tier. The *same* product can be allowable or prohibited for restricted data depending on the tier the organisation has. Chapter 6 operationalises this distinction.
- **Model providers change defaults silently.** Vendor-controlled model routing, context-length changes, and system-prompt changes happen outside the team's deploy cycle. The registry treats *the same model name* as a moving target and schedules re-evaluation on a quarterly cadence, with eval regressions from [`mod-103`](../mod-103-review-standards-and-eval-commissioning/) as the detection mechanism.
- **Build-vs-buy is rarely a build decision at team scope.** The R6 rubric scores the build-vs-buy threshold at 0.28 — below the threshold for building. The module keeps the question at team scope anyway because the *wrap* and *extend* stances (Chapter 3) are genuinely in scope and often correctly chosen; "buy with a thin extension layer" is a very different stance from "buy and use as shipped."

## Example: the request from a new engineer on Monday morning

A composite shaped example — calibration, not a case study.

A level-40 engineering-team manager at a mid-sized SaaS company has an eight-person team running Cursor Business + Claude Code + GitHub Copilot Enterprise. The tool registry exists; it is one quarter old. On a Monday morning, a newly-hired engineer — joining from a company where the whole team had standardised on Windsurf — pings the manager with two questions:

> "Two things: (1) can I get a Windsurf seat? I'm faster in it than in Cursor. (2) I've been using the Devin agent with my old team for refactors; can I turn it on for ours? I'll put it on my personal AmEx until expensing gets sorted."

The manager reads this and immediately sees the three failure modes this module exists to prevent:

- **Default-tool drift.** Giving one engineer a Windsurf seat because they are faster in it is reasonable in isolation. Across twelve such reasonable one-offs the team ends up with five IDE plugins, three agent CLIs, and no answer to "which tools saw the codebase this quarter."
- **Expense-report shadow procurement.** "On my personal AmEx until expensing gets sorted" is shadow IT. Shadow IT is a data-governance incident waiting to happen (see [`mod-109`](../mod-109-accountability-incident-response-and-blameless-postmortems/)): the organisation has no DPA with the vendor, no data-class fit assessment, and no opt-out verification. The IC will paste proprietary code into the vendor anyway, because the IC is trying to do their job.
- **Agent-authoring-environment lock without evaluation.** Standing up a third agent environment on top of Claude Code and Cursor is fine *if* the registry supports it. If the IC's prior experience is the only data point, the decision is a preference, not a selection.

The manager does not answer Monday morning. The manager replies Monday afternoon:

> "Good signals. The quarterly registry review runs in six weeks; let's queue both of these for it. For now: (1) Windsurf is on our evaluation list already — I'll pull your experience in as pilot evidence against our Chapter 2 bake-off rubric; let's book 30 min to score it. (2) Devin — don't put it on personal cards, we'll end up in a data-governance mess. Let's open a vendor-eval request through procurement so we have a path to a compliant trial. In the meantime, your refactor workflow runs on Claude Code; if it hits a limit, name the limit and that is Chapter 2 evidence."

Two weeks later the IC has filled a one-page bake-off form on Windsurf (output-quality judgement on six representative tasks, latency observations, data-handling clause verification). The manager pulls that evidence into the registry review. Six weeks later the registry adds Windsurf as a *per-IC optional* tool (per the standardise-vs-let-ICs-pick framing from Chapter 4), budget-neutral because Windsurf is swapped for the IC's Cursor seat rather than added to it. Devin is put through a procurement-initiated eval that takes eight weeks; by the end of it, the organisation has a DPA, the team has a tier-one data-class position, and the IC is using it on a compliant contract.

The deliverable the manager produced on Monday was not a tool decision. The deliverable was a *path to a decision* that routed demand into the registry. The registry is what makes this possible.

## Summary

- Team-scope vendor selection is **operational**, not programmatic: the manager picks, budgets, due-diligences, and sunsets *within* the enterprise framework that procurement and [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning) own above. The manager is the smallest unit at which vendor choice becomes real behaviour.
- The failure modes the module prevents: **default-tool drift**, **early-lock enterprise commit**, **governance theatre**. The composed deliverable — the **tool registry** — is the single artifact that keeps the team out of all three.
- **Five artifacts over the module**: [copilot evaluation matrix](exercises/exercise-01-copilot-build-vs-buy-matrix.md), [agent framework matrix](exercises/exercise-02-agent-framework-selection-matrix.md), [budget distribution plan](exercises/exercise-03-tool-budget-and-token-budget-distribution.md), [due-diligence checklist](exercises/exercise-04-vendor-due-diligence-checklist.md), [DPA / SOC 2 posture review](exercises/exercise-05-data-handling-dpa-and-soc2-posture-review.md), composed into one registry in Chapter 7.
- **Scope boundary**: not procurement (upward to [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning)); not vendor security assessment (sideways to SecOps); not FinOps commitment architecture (sideways to FinOps); not legal opinion (out of scope to counsel); not inference-infrastructure build (sideways to `ai-infra-engineer` / `model-platform-engineer`).
- **The live market shape**: unconsolidated copilot landscape, several serious agent frameworks, enterprise-tier contracts materially distinct from consumer tier, silent default changes by vendors, build-vs-buy rarely a build decision but "wrap" and "extend" genuinely in scope.
