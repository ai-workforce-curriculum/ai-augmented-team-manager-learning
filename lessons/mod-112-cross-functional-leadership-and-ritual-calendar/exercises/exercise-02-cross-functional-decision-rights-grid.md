# Exercise 02 — Cross-Functional Decision-Rights Grid

**Estimated effort:** 2 hours.

## Objective

Produce **v0.1 of your team's cross-functional decision-rights grid**: for each of the five partner functions in scope at L40 (Product, Platform / DevEx, Design, Data / Analytics, AI-Governance peer), a populated RAPID-style table of joint decision types mapped to Recommend / Agree / Perform / Input / Decide roles and to the ritual on the Chapter 2 calendar that executes the decision.

The grid is the space-layer of the team operating system (Chapter 7). The methodological anchor is [Chapter 3](../03-cross-functional-partnerships-and-decision-rights.md) for the five partner functions, the RAPID framing, and the Google re:Work Project Oxygen behavioural baseline that the grid reinforces.

## Problem statement

The team the L40 manager owns sits inside a cross-functional surface. Product owns the *what*; Platform / DevEx owns the *where and how*; Design owns the *patterns*; Data owns the *metric definitions*; the AI-Governance peer owns the *engineered-risk and governance rails*. When the joints are undefined, decisions are made twice (once in each room), or not made at all, or made in a crisis Zoom with the wrong people. The grid replaces this with a written structure: for each likely joint decision, who recommends, who agrees, who performs, who contributes input, and *who decides* — the last slot held by exactly one named role.

The failure mode the exercise prevents: a grid drafted by the manager alone, in good faith, that the partner has never signed off on. Such a grid is a projection of the manager's preferences, not a contract; the first time it is invoked under pressure it fails and partnership trust is damaged. A grid drafted *with* each partner, with a 30-minute conversation per partnership, is a contract and holds under pressure.

Scope: this exercise covers the five first-order partnerships at L40 — Product, Platform / DevEx, Design, Data / Analytics, AI-Governance peer. Upward partnerships (direct manager, [`ai-enablement-lead`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-enablement-lead-learning), [`senior-ai-governance-architect`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/senior-ai-governance-architect-learning), [`head-of-ai-governance`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/head-of-ai-governance-learning)) are *consumers* of the grid via the pre-read and are not themselves grid rows; see Chapter 4 for the upward composition and Chapter 7 for the full ownership boundary.

## Steps

1. **Identify the five partner roles by named person.** For each of the five partner functions, write down the specific person who holds the role in your organisation. If a role is unfilled or indirect (e.g., Design is served by one designer rotating from a central team), note it; the grid still gets drafted against the role, with the specific person to be named when confirmed.
2. **Enumerate the likely joint decision types per partnership.** For each partnership, list 4–8 decision types that have been made, or likely will be made, jointly. Chapter 3 provides worked examples for Product; replicate the shape for each of the other four. Draw on the last six months of actual joint decisions to ground the enumeration — abstract lists tend to miss the specifics.
3. **Draft the grid for each partnership.** Per Chapter 3's template:
   | Decision type | Recommend | Agree | Perform | Input | Decide | Ritual |
   For each row, fill all seven columns. The Decide column must have exactly one named role; joint-decide is a smell and gets resolved by naming the tie-breaker.
4. **Identify the ritual on the Chapter 2 calendar that executes each row.** If no current ritual fits, note the gap — either the grid row is wrong, or the calendar needs a new ritual. Resolve in step 7.
5. **Run a 30-minute conversation per partnership with the named partner.** Walk the draft grid together. The partner will likely:
   - Suggest decision types you missed.
   - Suggest decision types that should not be joint and are properly single-function.
   - Push back on specific Decide slots.
   - Identify partnership behaviours that are working and should be codified.
   The grid gets amended live in the conversation. The output of the conversation is a grid the partner would sign.
6. **Reconcile with the Google re:Work Project Oxygen baseline.** For each partnership, check that the structural grid is reinforcing — not replacing — the behavioural layer. Specifically: are the rituals in step 4 structured so that *is a good communicator*, *collaborates across*, *is a good coach*, and *has key technical skills* behaviours have room? A grid full of formalism and no coaching time has over-structured; a grid with rituals but no decision-rights has under-structured.
7. **Address calendar gaps.** For each grid row with no ritual executor, propose a change: add the row to an existing ritual, create a new ritual (and run it through the Exercise 01 calendar-audit discipline before adding), or reclassify the decision as handled off-calendar (with explicit cadence rules in the grid).
8. **Write a decision-log entry for the v0.1 grid per partnership.** Per Chapter 5's ADR-style template: context, decision (the signed grid itself as a linked attachment), rationale, alternatives, reversal conditions (e.g., "if a quarter passes with no decisions landing cleanly in the expected ritual, revisit"), review date (default: annual, with event triggers).
9. **Publish the grids.** One canonical page per partnership, linked from the team one-pager (Chapter 5) and the ritual calendar (Chapter 2). Partners each have a copy.
10. **Validate against a recent decision.** Pick one joint decision that was made in the last three months. Walk the grid: did the decision land in the ritual the grid predicts? Was the Decide role the one named? If the grid predicts something different from what happened, either the grid is wrong (amend) or the behaviour is wrong (fix). The validation is how you know the grid is not fiction.

## Starter guidance

- **The grid is drafted *with* the partner, not unilaterally.** A grid the partner has not seen is a wish-list. The 30-minute conversation per partnership is the load-bearing step of this exercise.
- **One Decide slot per row.** Joint-decide invites retroactive disagreement. Name the tie-breaker — usually the function whose scope is more affected.
- **The grid is dated and lives in the decision log.** Partnership structures shift; when they shift, the shift is a decision, not an undocumented drift.
- **Not every joint discussion is a grid row.** Grid rows are *decisions*, not conversations. A standing coordination sync between teams is a *ritual* on the calendar; the decisions made at it are grid rows.
- **The AI-Governance peer grid may be with an L25 `ai-risk-engineer`.** In organisations with no formal governance peer at team scope, the engineered-risk relationship from [`mod-109` Ch. 5](../../mod-109-accountability-incident-response-and-blameless-postmortems/05-routing-contract-with-risk-engineer-and-secops.md) is the grid's basis.
- **Platform / DevEx often does not have a co-equal peer at L40.** The grid may be between the manager and an L50 Platform EM. That is normal; the grid is a peer-partnership artifact even when the counterparts are at different levels.
- **Avoid the "manager decides everything on the team" trap.** A grid where the manager is the Decide role on every row is a sign the grid was drafted without the partner. Partnership decisions that concern the partner's domain often belong to the partner.
- **Avoid the "partner decides everything that touches the partner" trap.** The opposite failure mode. The manager holds the Decide slot on team-internal workflow decisions even when the partner is interested.
- **Rituals that execute multiple rows are efficient.** The quarterly roadmap sync may execute 3–5 Product-partnership grid rows; the governance packet sync may execute 2–3 Governance-partnership rows. One ritual per row is over-structuring.
- **Dormant rows are okay.** A grid row that predicts a decision that has not emerged in the first quarter is not necessarily wrong; the decision may be rare. Mark it dormant in the annual retro; retire only if dormancy persists.

## Acceptance criteria

The grid is complete when:

- A populated grid exists for each of the five partner functions (Product, Platform / DevEx, Design, Data / Analytics, AI-Governance peer), with 4–8 decision-type rows each.
- Every row has a filled Recommend / Agree / Perform / Input / Decide column, with exactly one Decide role per row.
- Every row names the ritual (from the Chapter 2 calendar) that executes the decision; calendar gaps have been addressed.
- Each partnership's grid was drafted or amended in a joint 30-minute conversation with the named partner. The partner would sign the grid.
- A decision-log entry per partnership captures context, decision, rationale, alternatives, reversal conditions, review date, links.
- The grids are published at canonical locations and linked from the team one-pager and the ritual calendar.
- A validation step against one recent decision confirms the grid predicts the actual decision path (or, if it does not, the grid or the behaviour has been amended).
- Dormant rows are marked explicitly; the annual retro is scheduled.
- A change-log entry names authors (manager + partner), reviewers, and the date; v0.1.

## Stretch goals

- **RAPID coverage view.** Across all five partnerships, compute the count of Decide roles held by each named person. If one person holds Decide on >50% of total rows, the partnership pattern is centralised; discuss with partners whether the distribution matches intent.
- **Decision-log backfill.** Review the last six months of major team-scope decisions and retroactively populate decision-log entries for the ones that were material enough to warrant one. The backfill reveals partnership decisions that got made informally and tests whether the grid predicts them.
- **Partnership-health instrument.** For each partnership, after one quarter of operating the grid, run a short joint survey: "Did decisions land in the ritual the grid predicted? Was the Decide role respected? Was the cadence right?" Use the results to inform the annual retro amendment.
- **Boundary stress test.** Pick one decision type that *crosses two partnerships* (e.g., a tooling decision that involves Platform / DevEx and AI-Governance). Walk the grids with both partners. Resolve any conflict in the Decide slot explicitly rather than leaving the boundary fuzzy.
- **Cross-reference with the pre-read (Exercise 03).** The decisions that feed the quarterly pre-read's "ask" section should be grid rows where Decide is above the manager. If asks are routing to the wrong level, the grid is predicting the wrong escalation; amend.
