# Quiz 01 — Cross-Functional Leadership and the Team Ritual Calendar

**Format:** 11 multiple-choice questions.
**Coverage:** All seven chapters of mod-112.
**Scoring:** 1 point per question. 8/11 is a pass. Below that, re-read the chapter named in the answer explanation before moving on.

Each question tests whether you can apply the chapter to a specific team-scope situation the manager actually faces — not whether you can quote it. Pick the single best answer.

---

## Question 1

An L40 manager has produced all eleven mod-101 to mod-111 artifacts. Each is individually defensible. Their new VP asks: *"What is your team's operating system?"* Per Chapter 1, the strongest answer is:

- A. The eleven backing artifacts themselves, posted to the team wiki with a table of contents.
- B. **The composition — a ritual calendar that runs the artifacts on cadence, a decision-rights map that locates joint decisions, an upward communication posture, a one-pager, and a decision log — plus the practices that produce and maintain those artifacts.** Chapter 1 is explicit: *"The operating system is not a document. It is the set of practices that produce and maintain those five artifacts. The artifacts are the evidence the operating system is running; the practices are the system itself."* Eleven backing artifacts only generate value when someone runs them on cadence, routes information between them, surfaces outputs to partners and leadership, and keeps the whole stack legible to the next person who will own it. The three failure modes mod-112 prevents — *calendar rot*, *cross-functional ambiguity*, *upward illegibility* — all share one underlying cause: eleven defensible artifacts without a composition layer compose into nothing. The whole module optimises for the Monday-morning successor test; what the test reads is the composition, not the backing artifacts in isolation.
- C. The weekly all-hands plus the quarterly off-site review.
- D. The [`mod-104`](../../mod-104-productivity-measurement-and-ai-roi/) AI-ROI dashboard, which rolls up signals from the other modules.

## Question 2

A team runs a weekly 60-minute "team meeting": the first 20 minutes resolve this week's blockers; the next 20 minutes discuss next quarter's strategy; the last 20 minutes are open discussion. The manager considers the slot "efficient." Per Chapter 2's Lencioni classification, the slot is:

- A. A model weekly tactical — strategic agenda breadth is a feature, not a bug.
- B. **Overloaded: the slot serves two incompatible Lencioni types at once (weekly tactical and monthly strategic) and must be split or re-scoped.** Chapter 2 is explicit: *"Every ritual on the calendar is one of the four types; a ritual that is more than one is a sign the slot is overloaded and should be split."* The chapter names the specific mis-classification: *"Weekly tactical pretending to be monthly strategic. 'We'll discuss the roadmap this week' — one weekly tactical in a quarter does not do the work of a monthly strategic session. The roadmap discussion needs its own slot or it does not happen."* Twenty minutes of strategy time embedded in a weekly tactical starves strategic deliberation without delivering strategic output; the open-discussion third has no classifiable purpose at all. The fix is to split: keep the weekly tactical for current-week blockers; promote the roadmap work to a monthly strategic slot with real deliberation time; retire the open-discussion third or move it to office hours.
- C. An efficient use of time — Lencioni's framework is prescriptive, not a classifier the team is required to apply.
- D. A monthly strategic that happens to run weekly — the cadence is the only thing to fix.

## Question 3

A manager runs the annual calendar audit (Exercise 01) on the Wednesday 90-minute "AI tools sync" that has sat on the calendar for a year. The last four sessions have been vendor-release-note demos plus general chat; stage distribution on the [`mod-105`](../../mod-105-team-ai-enablement-programs/) ladder has been stable for two quarters; the defended tile (*"novel-workflow adoption rate"*) has not moved. The annual cost is ~384 person-hr/yr. Per Chapter 2, the disciplined audit outcome is:

- A. Keep the ritual — it is cheap to run and might become useful again next quarter.
- B. Retire the ritual outright and delete the slot — the signal it defended is clearly no longer at risk.
- C. **Right-size: retire the weekly 60-minute slot, replace with a monthly 45-minute CoP session with a named owner, an explicit per-session purpose, a reversal condition that revives the weekly cadence if the defended tile regresses or stage progression drops, and a dated decision-log entry capturing context, rationale, alternatives, reversal conditions, and review date.** Chapter 2's worked example uses almost exactly this scenario and names the pattern: *"Retirement is usually right-sizing, not elimination: the quarterly AI tools sync that was weekly in Q1 is a normal shape change, not a failure of the ritual."* The audit decision must land in the decision log with the Chapter 5 template — a log entry that says *"we don't do standups anymore"* is not a decision, it is a change-of-state note. The capacity reclaimed (~288 person-hr/yr in Chapter 2's example) is a number that lands on the next quarterly pre-read's productivity section.
- D. Convert the ritual to async in Slack — the content is low-value enough that synchronous time is wasted but the forum is "still needed."

## Question 4

A manager drafts the Product-partnership decision-rights grid alone over a weekend, publishes it to the team wiki, and tags the PM. Three rows list manager and PM as *joint Decide*; a fourth lists the VP Product as Decide but has never been discussed with the VP. Per Chapter 3, the grid is:

- A. A reasonable v0.1 — the PM will push back on anything they disagree with asynchronously.
- B. **A wish-list, not a contract — and the joint-Decide rows invite retroactive disagreement.** Chapter 3 is explicit on both failures. On unilateral drafting: *"The grid is drafted by the manager and the partner together — not by the manager unilaterally. A grid the partner has not seen is a wish-list; a grid the partner has signed is a contract. The drafting conversation is often the most valuable session of the module: it surfaces decisions that have been getting made informally and locates them."* On joint-Decide: *"One Decide slot per row. Joint decide (two names) is a smell: in practice one of them will decide, and the ambiguity invites retroactive disagreement. Write down which of the two is the tie-breaker."* The fourth row — naming the VP Product as Decide without having spoken to them — is a decision made *about* someone's authority *without* them, which is a Chapter 1 cross-functional-ambiguity failure mode in miniature. The fix is a 30-minute joint drafting conversation per partnership: amended live, tie-breakers named for every row, VP-escalation rows confirmed with the VP before publication.
- C. Correct — the joint-Decide rows show healthy partnership trust.
- D. A model grid, provided the manager watches for the PM's reaction in Slack.

## Question 5

A new L40 manager is proud of their Google re:Work Project Oxygen fluency: they coach each direct report weekly, they are a good communicator, they collaborate across the org, they have the technical skills to advise the team. They have not drafted the Chapter 3 decision-rights grid. Mid-quarter a joint Product / Design decision gets made twice, in incompatible ways, in different rooms. Per Chapter 3, the diagnosis is:

- A. Project Oxygen is insufficient as a model; the manager needs a different behavioural framework.
- B. **The behavioural baseline alone does not scale under cross-function pressure; Project Oxygen behaviours need the structural scaffolding of the decision-rights grid and the partner-ritual cadence to reinforce them — structure and behaviour compose; neither replaces the other.** Chapter 3's thesis: *"Project Oxygen is the behavioural baseline; decision-rights grids and partner-ritual cadence are the structural scaffolding that makes those behaviours scale across a five-function partnership surface."* And: *"The grid without the behaviours is a bureaucratic artifact; the behaviours without the grid scale only until the partnership is tested; together they produce partnerships that run on cadence, resolve joint decisions cleanly, and compose upward."* The manager's coaching, communicating, and collaborating are still correct; they simply lack the structural layer that locates a joint decision in a known room with a named Decide role, which is exactly why the same decision got made twice in different rooms.
- C. The manager over-relied on Design; the fix is to pull more decisions in-team.
- D. The decision got made in Slack; the fix is a Slack-channel policy.

## Question 6

A manager drafts a Q3 pre-read with four spines: a roadmap-and-outcome section, a productivity-and-ROI section, an incidents-and-governance section, and a vendor-registry section — each clean, each defensible, each about 1.5 pages. The VP reads them individually and makes decisions that are locally reasonable but together pull the team in incoherent directions. Per Chapter 4, the failure mode is:

- A. The pre-read is too short — more connective tissue in each section would fix it.
- B. **Four-separate-reports syndrome: four defensible reports, one incoherent read by leadership because the pre-read has four spines rather than one.** Chapter 4 names it exactly: *"The manager produces four separate reports: productivity, incidents, governance, vendor spend. Each is clean. Leadership reads them separately; the connective tissue — the incidents are retrospected because the governance posture requires it; the spend is distributed because the enablement stages require it; the productivity is what it is because the roadmap-and-adoption curve shaped it — is invisible. Leadership makes locally reasonable decisions from each report that are globally incoherent for the team."* The fix is the composition discipline: *"The roadmap is the spine. The quarter is organised around what the team committed to and delivered. The productivity, governance, and incident sections are in service of the roadmap narrative."* A pre-read with four spines is four pre-reads stapled together; the single-spine composition — productivity reads the spine, governance protects the spine, vendor funds the spine, people populates the spine — is the whole point of the chapter.
- C. The VP is reading badly — a pre-read should not need composition work at the reader's end.
- D. The pre-read is too long — trimming each section by a third would solve it.

## Question 7

An L40 manager drafts their Q4 pre-read in the voice of a board report: *"The firm's AI posture remains appropriate for the regulatory environment; material risk is adequately mitigated; the AI program delivered X% of portfolio objectives this quarter."* They add a signature line reading *"on behalf of the AI program."* Per Chapter 4, the pre-read is:

- A. Correct — serious leadership language is the right register for an executive audience.
- B. Correct — the register is a legitimate way to signal readiness for an L60 promotion track.
- C. **Over-reach: the L40 pre-read is board-adjacent, not board-level; board-level narrative lives at [`head-of-ai-governance`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/head-of-ai-governance-learning) (L60) and [`chief-ai-officer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/chief-ai-officer-learning) (L70); the language register and the "on behalf of the AI program" signature both signal an attempt to write someone else's narrative.** Chapter 4 is explicit about the register: *"The L40 pre-read uses precise, specific language — 'the team shipped X', 'we routed Y to counsel'. Writing in board-level register at L40 is a signal of over-reach that leadership will read as noise."* And the signature line: *"The L40 pre-read signs: manager, often PM, often governance peer. The board narrative signs: `chief-ai-officer`, with contributions from `head-of-ai-governance` and counsel. An L40 pre-read signed 'on behalf of the AI program' is over-reach."* The chapter also notes that board-register-at-L40 produces a document that is *"simultaneously too coarse for the team's own operation and too fine for the board's attention, and is therefore useful to neither."* The fix is specific, single-team, precise language — what *the team* did — plus correct signatures.
- D. Correct provided the governance peer co-signs the board-register framing.

## Question 8

A decision-log entry reads, in full: *"DL-2026-10-03-002: Retired the Wednesday standup because we weren't getting value from it. — J. Doe, team manager."* Per Chapter 5, this entry is:

- A. A model decision-log entry — dated, authored, action-stating, concise.
- B. **Unreadable to a successor: it has a date and an action but no context, no rationale citing the Chapter 2 audit, no alternatives considered, no reversal conditions, and no review date.** Chapter 5 is explicit on the ADR-style template fields a material decision needs: *"ID, Title, Date, Author, Status, Scope, Context, Decision, Rationale, Alternatives considered, Reversal conditions, Review date, Links."* The chapter names this exact failure: *"A log of 'we decided X on date Y' without why X and what would reverse it is a log that cannot be read. The successor sees that a decision was made; they do not know whether the conditions that justified it still hold."* The successor-readiness check — *"Read the last six months of decision log entries in 30 minutes and understand the team's recent history: what changed, why it changed, what would reverse it, when to revisit"* — is specifically failed by entries without reversal conditions and review dates. The fix is the full ADR template (context citing audit, decision stated, alternatives weighed, reversal conditions dated, links to the Chapter 2 template and the replacement ritual's charter).
- C. Adequate — the manager's authority is the signal a successor trusts.
- D. Over-documented — a retirement is trivial enough that one line suffices.

## Question 9

A manager's team one-pager has a glowing executive-summary bullet (*"team delivered 95% of the Q3 roadmap with a 22% productivity gain year-over-year through AI adoption"*), an empty open-loops section, and no "what the team is not" section. Per Chapter 5, this one-pager:

- A. Is a model one-pager — concise, outcome-focused, no clutter.
- B. Is correct for the current quarter — the empty open-loops section is an honest signal of healthy steady state.
- C. **Violates all three honesty disciplines Chapter 5 names.** The chapter is explicit on each: *"Open loops section cannot be empty. A team with no open loops is either at an unusual moment of steady state (one quarter per year, if ever) or is hiding things. Peers who see an empty open-loops section stop trusting the document."* And: *"What the team is not section exists. A team that does not know what it is not has not scoped itself. Peers reading this section learn what not to escalate to the team, which is more useful than learning what to escalate."* And: *"No AI-ROI headline number without the qualifier. If the one-pager cites a productivity gain, cite the [`mod-104` Ch. 1](../../mod-104-productivity-measurement-and-ai-roi/01-metrics-that-separate-gain-from-theatre.md) anti-theatre framing in one sub-clause… '22% faster with AI' is marketing and will be read as marketing."* The fix adds honest driver attribution to the productivity line (roadmap, enablement, tooling, external drivers separated from AI assist), repopulates open loops with three to five dated items with named owners, and adds the scope-boundary sentence. Marketing-shaped one-pagers teach peers to read *around* them rather than *from* them — which is exactly the trust the Chapter 5 honesty discipline exists to preserve.
- D. Is correct provided the dashboard URL in the measurement-anchor section covers the attribution detail.

## Question 10

An L40 manager wants to upgrade their cross-functional vocabulary. Their organisation has a mature privacy function (counsel holds CIPP/E and the DPO is a privacy specialist), is *not* pursuing ISO/IEC 42001 certification, and is multi-cloud (no single cloud provider is primary). Per Chapter 6, the correct portfolio shape is:

- A. Pursue CIPP/E first to align with counsel's vocabulary.
- B. Pursue AIGP + ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer + CIPP/E + AWS Certified AI Practitioner in parallel — comprehensive coverage is the goal.
- C. **Pursue AIGP as the primary anchor; defer ISO/IEC 42001 (not load-bearing without an AIMS commitment); defer CIPP/E (duplicates counsel's own credential); decline the cloud credential (platform-team territory; no primary cloud).** Chapter 6 names AIGP as the primary default anchor explicitly: *"[IAPP AIGP](https://iapp.org/certify/aigp/) is the primary default anchor… ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer is load-bearing only when the organisation has committed to AIMS."* And on portfolio sizing: *"The portfolio is narrow: typically AIGP as default, plus one or two situational credentials maximum. A portfolio of six credentials signals credentialism rather than operational competence."* The three-question rubric from Chapter 6 leads to exactly this shape: *primary partner's shared language* (governance peer and counsel reference AIGP body-of-knowledge concepts) → AIGP; *load-bearing in the organisation* (no AIMS, no single cloud) → no ISO/42001, no cloud credential; *opportunity cost* (counsel already holds CIPP/E) → do not duplicate. The manager's goal is *shared vocabulary with partners*, not *comprehensive personal certification*.
- D. Decline all four — certifications are resume theatre and change nothing about running practice.

## Question 11

An L40 manager has run mod-112 for a year and is proud of their composed operating system. At the quarterly cross-team EM guild, they volunteer to draft *"the program's AI-governance architecture"* and *"the firm's board-level AI narrative for next quarter."* Per Chapter 7, the manager is:

- A. Showing initiative appropriate to L40 career development; the module's capstone is writing the upward composition.
- B. **Over-reaching into layers the L40 role does not own: program-governance architecture is [`senior-ai-governance-architect`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/senior-ai-governance-architect-learning) (L50) scope; the board-level narrative is [`head-of-ai-governance`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/head-of-ai-governance-learning) (L60) and [`chief-ai-officer`](https://github.com/ai-governance-curriculum/chief-ai-officer-learning) (L70) scope.** Chapter 7's ownership boundary is explicit: *"The manager owns composition at team scope. Composition means: the eleven backing artifacts, the ritual calendar that runs them, the partnerships that decide across them, the pre-read that narrates them upward. Composition is the manager's distinctive responsibility — no one else holds it at this level. The manager does not own the layers above. The board narrative, the AIMS program, the enterprise procurement framework, the engineering-manager-of-managers coordination, the policy architecture — these are upward responsibilities that consume the manager's composed artifacts. The manager who tries to own them is over-reaching; the manager who makes the artifacts consumable is doing their L40 job well."* The correct action is to make the team's pre-read, one-pager, and decision log exemplary so the L50 / L60 roles can compose them cleanly into their program-level and board-level artifacts — not to attempt to produce those upward artifacts in parallel.
- C. Correct — the capstone synthesis chapter of the module is writing the upward program narrative.
- D. Correct provided they clear the scope with their VP first; VP approval would convert the volunteer into an authorised assignment.

1. **B.** Chapter 1's *"What a team operating system means at this scope"* and *"The six pieces of mod-112"* are explicit that the operating system is the composition — not the eleven backing artifacts, not any one ritual, not the dashboard. The three failure modes (calendar rot, cross-functional ambiguity, upward illegibility) and the Monday-morning successor test both point to composition as the deliverable. (Chapter 1, *"Motivation"*, *"What a team operating system means at this scope"*, and *"The Monday-morning successor test"*.)

2. **B.** Chapter 2's *"Lencioni's four meeting types"* names the exact mis-classification and prescribes the fix: *"Every ritual on the calendar is one of the four types; a ritual that is more than one is a sign the slot is overloaded and should be split."* The 20-minutes-of-strategy embedded in a weekly tactical is Chapter 2's textbook *"Weekly tactical pretending to be monthly strategic"* pattern. (Chapter 2, *"Lencioni's four meeting types"*, especially the common mis-classifications list.)

3. **C.** Chapter 2's worked example *"A calendar audit that retires a ritual"* uses almost this scenario and lands exactly on right-sizing with a dated decision-log entry naming replacement, reversal condition, review date, and reclaimed-capacity number. Retirement is usually right-sizing, not elimination; the Chapter 5 ADR template is the shape the decision log entry takes. (Chapter 2, *"Example: a calendar audit that retires a ritual"* and *"The audit cadence"*; Chapter 5 template.)

4. **B.** Chapter 3 is explicit on both failures: unilateral drafting produces a wish-list rather than a contract; joint-Decide rows invite retroactive disagreement and require a named tie-breaker. The fourth row violates both patterns — the VP has not agreed to the authority the grid assigns them. (Chapter 3, *"The decision-rights grid"*, specifically the two design principles.)

5. **B.** Chapter 3's thesis is that Project Oxygen is the *behavioural* baseline and decision-rights grids plus partner-ritual cadence are the *structural* scaffolding. Behaviour without structure stops scaling under cross-function pressure; the same-decision-made-twice-in-two-rooms is the canonical cross-function-pressure failure. (Chapter 3, *"The Google re:Work behavioural baseline"* and *"Motivation"*.)

6. **B.** Chapter 4 names *"Four-separate-reports syndrome"* as one of the three failure modes the pre-read chapter exists to prevent and prescribes the single-spine composition discipline — usually roadmap-to-outcome — with all other sections in service of the spine. *"A pre-read with four spines is four pre-reads stapled together, which is the failure mode this chapter names."* (Chapter 4, *"Motivation"* and *"The composition discipline — connecting the four streams"*.)

7. **C.** Chapter 4's *"The board-adjacent / board-level boundary"* makes the register explicit: precise, specific, single-team at L40; aggregated, strategic, firm-position at L60 / L70. The "on behalf of the AI program" signature is specifically named as over-reach. Writing in board register at L40 produces a document useful to neither the team's own operation nor the board's attention. (Chapter 4, *"The board-adjacent / board-level boundary"* and *"What the pre-read is not"*.)

8. **B.** Chapter 5's ADR-style template lists the required fields for a material-decision log entry and the chapter explicitly names the failure of a log without rationale and reversal conditions: *"The successor sees that a decision was made; they do not know whether the conditions that justified it still hold."* The successor-readiness check requires the peer to read six months of entries in thirty minutes and understand not just what changed but why and under what conditions it would change back. (Chapter 5, *"The decision log"* and *"The successor-readiness check"*.)

9. **C.** Chapter 5's *"The honesty discipline for the one-pager"* names all three disciplines the one-pager violates — non-empty open-loops section, *what the team is not* section present, AI-ROI claims qualified with honest driver attribution. Marketing-shaped one-pagers teach peers to read around rather than from them; the honesty discipline exists to preserve the artifact's readability over quarters. (Chapter 5, *"The honesty discipline for the one-pager"*.)

10. **C.** Chapter 6 names AIGP as the primary default anchor; names ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer as load-bearing *only* when the organisation has committed to AIMS; the three-question portfolio rubric steers away from duplicating counsel's own credential (CIPP/E held by counsel and DPO) and away from a cloud credential when no single cloud is primary. The worked example at a mid-sized European SaaS company lands at the same portfolio shape. *"The portfolio is narrow: typically AIGP as default, plus one or two situational credentials maximum."* (Chapter 6, *"IAPP AIGP as the primary vocabulary anchor"*, *"ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer as the secondary anchor"*, *"The portfolio-planning rubric"*, and the worked example.)

11. **B.** Chapter 7's ownership-boundary table and the three lines beneath it are explicit: the manager owns composition at team scope; program-governance architecture is L50; the board narrative is L60 / L70. *"The manager who tries to own them is over-reaching; the manager who makes the artifacts consumable is doing their L40 job well."* VP approval does not reassign ownership — the artifacts belong to other roles by level, not by permission. (Chapter 7, *"The ownership boundary — precisely"* and *"What this module — and this track — do not produce"*.)
